# Cyber research & media landscape — where The Decision Files fits

**Date:** 2026-08-15 · **Status:** research note
**Why:** before building a research practice in security, know who already owns which ground — and whether the media "friend" is actually an independent party.

---

## The finding that changes the Shimel conversation

**Futurum Group acquired Techstrong Group in April 2024.** Alan Shimel announced it himself
on DevOps.com. Techstrong — DevOps.com, Security Boulevard, Techstrong TV, the events and
lead-gen engines — is now inside an analyst firm.

**Futurum is not a media company. It's analyst + media + a testing lab:**

- Analyst/advisory research
- Techstrong media properties and distribution
- **Signal65** — lab and product testing
- Tech Field Day

**That is the NSS-plus-media model, already assembled at scale.** It is the closest existing
thing to what The Decision Files would grow into.

### What it means, honestly

**Alan is not an independent friend with a podcast.** He's an executive inside a firm with an
adjacent P&L that already monetizes research, syndication, and reprints. That doesn't kill the
collaboration — it changes it from a favor between friends into a business conversation, and
raises what's on the table.

Three ways it can go, and they're not exclusive:

1. **Partner.** Futurum has distribution and an analyst brand; a rigorous qualitative
   demand-side practice is a genuine gap in their stack (see the white space below).
2. **Customer.** Futurum is a research business with a production problem — at scale. That is
   precisely the "UpSight as production tooling for research firms" thesis, and Futurum is a
   far bigger version of it than IT-Harvest.
3. **Acquirer, eventually.** Worth knowing the shape of the board even if it's not the plan.

**The risk to name:** pitch a research practice to a firm that owns an analyst business, and
you're showing your method to someone structurally capable of running it. Lead with the
*tooling* and the *partnership*, not with "here's my whole methodology."

## The archive question — Rick's skepticism is right, and the reason matters

Rick's instinct: *"the archive could be gold or not, i suspect he talks to a lot of vendors."*

**Correct, and it's the deciding factor.** Techstrong/Security Boulevard programming is
industry-expert and vendor-executive interviews — supply-side content. Vendors describing their
own products is close to worthless for demand-side buyer research. You cannot extract "how
buyers decided" from a corpus of vendors explaining why their product is good.

**There is a second-order asset in it, but it's smaller than a first pass suggests.**

An archive of hundreds of vendors describing their own category across five-plus years is a
**category-evolution dataset** — "here is how 200 vendors described this category in 2021 versus
2026." Interesting, and nobody has run it.

**Correction (Rick's push-back, and he's right): historical drift is not monetizable.** Nobody
pays for history. A vendor can't act on "you renamed yourself twice since 2021." Futurum's own
analysts could write that retrospective from memory. And a media business monetizes attention —
sponsorship, events, lead gen — so a retrospective is one good article, not a product line.

**So the archive is demo material, not a product.** Good demo material: it's his data, at a scale
that proves the tool, surfacing something he didn't notice while living through it. That's all,
and that's enough for what it's for.

### What Futurum would actually pay for — and it isn't the archive

An analyst firm's binding constraint is the one Stiennon named: too many vendors, not enough
analyst hours. Futurum's analysts take **hundreds of vendor briefings a year**, recorded, which
then evaporate into one person's memory and some notes.

**Turning every incoming briefing into structured, searchable, comparable data is how an analyst
firm scales coverage without hiring analysts.** That's an existing labor cost and the bottleneck
on their core research product — a real budget line, not a content idea.

**The sequence:**
1. The archive analysis is the **proof** — *"here's what this did to five years of your own tapes
   in an afternoon."*
2. The **pitch** is the next twelve months — *"now every briefing your analysts take is queryable
   and comparable instead of dying in a notebook."*

Past drift earns the meeting. Forward briefing throughput is what gets bought.

**Two caveats.** It's an enterprise software sale to a company capable of building it themselves —
not fast money. And it pulls the Futurum relationship firmly into the *"UpSight as tooling for
research firms"* lane rather than the security research business. Worth noticing how often this
thread pulls that direction.

**What to verify before pitching:** the guest mix (what % vendor exec vs. practitioner), whether
transcripts exist in usable form, who owns them post-acquisition, and total volume.

## Reprint rights — Rick's instinct, and the numbers

**Reprint licensing runs roughly $25,000–$75,000 per report per year** for Gartner and Forrester
tier research. Not publicly listed; negotiated. This is why Magic Quadrant and Wave graphics
circulate freely in vendor marketing while the reports stay paywalled.

**This may be a better revenue line than the engagement.** It's per-report, annual, and sold to
*multiple* vendors off a single piece of research — where an engagement is one-to-one and
consumes Rick's hours. Line C in the business model was ranked "later, if ever." It should move
up.

Mechanics worth copying from Forrester's own client guide: reprints grant distribution of the
**original, unaltered** content only — no ownership, no edits, and a unique link per instance
rather than a posted PDF.

### The independence rules that make reprints safe

Reprints are where research businesses lose their credibility. Three hard rules:

1. **Publish first. Sell reprints second.** Never sell pre-publication. A vendor who can buy
   before publication can influence what gets published.
2. **A published rate card, available to any vendor mentioned.** Same price to everyone, favorable
   or unfavorable mention. This is what removes the appearance of pay-to-play.
3. **No sponsor touches subject selection, questions, or findings.** State it on the page.

The same rules apply to syndication on sponsored media properties — including Security Boulevard.
Get it explicit with Alan as an opening term, not a later correction.

## Who owns what ground

| Player | What they actually sell | Side |
|---|---|---|
| **Gartner** | Broad coverage, quadrants, inquiry. Losing clients — 14,149 (Q4'21) → 11,487 (Q1'26). | Supply |
| **Forrester** | Waves, quantitative buyer surveys, reprints | Supply + quant demand |
| **451 Research / S&P Global Market Intelligence** | Emerging-tech coverage, end-user surveys, forecast models, in-depth interviews. Scott Crawford leads InfoSec. Acquired by S&P 2019. | Supply + quant demand |
| **Omdia (Informa TechTarget)** | Analyst coverage; sister to Dark Reading | Supply |
| **Futurum + Techstrong + Signal65** | Analyst research + media + product testing lab | Supply + distribution |
| **IT-Harvest** | Comprehensive vendor data — 4,250 vendors, 547 in AI security. Advisory seats at ~1/10 Gartner price. | Supply |
| **Clozd, Primary Intelligence, DoubleCheck** | Private win/loss research for vendors | Demand — **never published** |

## The white space, stated plainly

**Published, qualitative, demand-side decision research.**

- The analyst firms do demand-side work, but it's **quantitative** — surveys, forecast models,
  n=400 with percentages. Not "here is how this named person actually decided."
- The win/loss firms do **qualitative demand-side** work, but it's **private** — a deliverable
  for one vendor, never published, so it builds no public authority.
- IT-Harvest and Futurum's lab are **supply-side** — vendors and products.

Nobody publishes rigorous qualitative research on how buyers actually decide. That is the gap,
it is real, and it is defensible for the same reason NSS's was: it requires candor, and candor
requires independence and relationships.

**The pitch line falls out of it:** *"Everyone counts buyers. Nobody asks them properly, in
public."*

## What to do with this

1. **Reprint rights move up the revenue stack** — from "later, if ever" to a primary line,
   modeled at $25–75K per report per year across multiple licensees.
2. **The Alan conversation is a Futurum conversation.** Three asks, in this order: syndication
   (cheap, fast), the archive as a category-evolution product built on UpSight (software sale),
   partnership on a demand-side practice (biggest, slowest, highest risk of showing your hand).
3. **Verify the archive before pitching it** — guest mix, transcript availability, ownership,
   volume.
4. **Do not lead with methodology** to a firm that owns an analyst business.
5. **Scott Crawford (S&P/451)** is a name worth adding to the map — the InfoSec research
   director at a firm doing both coverage and end-user research.
