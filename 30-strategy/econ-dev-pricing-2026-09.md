# Pricing and Packaging — Economic Development Lane

*Version: 1.0 (first draft) | Date: 2026-09-23 | Author: Rick Moy (with research agent)*
*Companion to `econ-dev-positioning-2026-09.md`, `econ-dev-data-rights-2026-09.md`,

> **This lane has zero customer evidence.** Not one EDC, chamber, city economic development
> office, SBDC or BID has told us what they would pay, what they can sign, or what they
> currently spend. No price in this document has been tested on a buyer. Every offer below is
> a **construction from published purchasing rules and published grant cost rules** — which is
> a much better basis than a guess, and still not the same thing as evidence.

> **Reading note on sourcing.** Per house rules, every table states whose numbers these are and
> whether they are **verified** (read on a primary source, URL and date given), **researched
> secondary** (real but read through an intermediary), or **modelled** (arithmetic built on
> stated assumptions — not a fact about the world). Nothing is left unlabelled. Where a number
> could not be verified, the cell says so in words rather than being left blank.

> **The governing order does not change here.** Grant reporting is the **wedge**. Early warning
> is the **retention story**. Pricing follows that order: the first invoice is attached to a
> filing deadline, the renewal is attached to what the corpus started showing.

## 0. The three constraints this pricing has to satisfy

Rick's direction, restated so the rest of the document can be checked against it:

1. **Price against approval thresholds.** Land under the amount one named person can sign
   without triggering competitive quotes, an RFP, or a board vote. **Being under the signature
   authority is worth more than a higher number.** This is the dominant constraint; where it
   conflicts with margin, it wins.
2. **A couple of pieces of business, not optimal business.** Reference customers and evidence
   beat margin in the next four quarters.
3. **Low-price bidder, paired with unique and turnkey.** Cheap-and-similar is a losing
   position. Cheap-and-nobody-else-does-this-and-you-do-nothing is a winning one. The price
   only works if the turnkey claim is literally true — see §3.4, where "what the agency does"
   is enumerated line by line.

Everything below is derived from those three plus one finding that arrived during the research
and reorganised the whole exercise: **§4.1. The money these buyers control mostly cannot be
spent on software.** It can be spent on a person's time. That is a packaging constraint before
it is a price constraint.

# A. THE THRESHOLD MAP

## A.1 Why this section is the core of the document

In a normal SaaS sale, price is a value question. In this lane price is first a **procedural**
question: at what number does a purchase stop being a decision one person makes and start being
a process several people run? Below that line a deal closes in weeks. Above it, a solo vendor
with no reference customers and no past performance record loses to incumbents, or to nobody, on
a twelve-month cycle he cannot finance.

So the price is set by the **lowest binding threshold across the targets we actually intend to
approach**, not by what the work is worth.

**Why this matters for nonprofits specifically.** An independent EDC or chamber is not bound by
municipal purchasing rules — but the moment it spends **federal pass-through money**, the
Uniform Guidance procurement standards at 2 CFR 200.318–200.327 bind it exactly as they bind a
city. SBA formally incorporated the Uniform Guidance for SBDCs in a 2023 final rule
([Federal Register, 7 Nov 2023](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/11/07/2023-22164/small-business-development-centers),
accessed 2026-09-23 — **verified**). So East County EDC and ABASD, as SBDC hosts, are running
federal funds through the same rulebook. **$15,000 is therefore the real ceiling for a
federally-funded first deal at a nonprofit**, not $50,000 — unless they have already
self-certified higher, which is a question to ask rather than an assumption to make.

**The Carlsbad caveat, because it matters and is easy to get wrong.** Carlsbad's $30,000 bid
limit is **CPI-indexed**: the code directs the purchasing officer to adjust it annually against
the San Diego–All Urban Consumers index in $5,000 increments, effective by memorandum at the
start of a fiscal year, cumulatively since FY2009. The $30,000 in the published code is the
base, not necessarily the current operative number, and the current number lives in an internal
memo rather than in the code. **The City Manager's $250,000 awarding authority is likewise
CPI-indexed beginning FY2027-28.** So: quote Carlsbad the code figure, and ask the purchasing
officer for the current memo. Do not tell a Carlsbad buyer "your limit is $30,000" as though it
were fixed.

## A.4 Nonprofit EDCs and chambers — what governs instead

Nonprofits are not bound by municipal purchasing codes. Three things bind them instead, in this
order of practical force:

1. **Funder rules on the money being spent.** If the dollars are federal pass-through (SBDC
   cooperative agreement, CDBG subaward, EDA award), 2 CFR 200.318–200.327 applies and the
   micro-purchase threshold is $15,000 unless they have self-certified higher. If the dollars
   are state (CalOSBA TAP, RII), the grant agreement's own cost rules apply — and those are
   tighter than procurement rules in ways that change the product, not just the price (§4).
2. **Board-adopted contract and spending policy.** Typically an executive-director signature
   limit above which board approval is required, frequently paired with a term limit (e.g. any
   contract over $X or longer than 12 months). **These are internal documents. They are not
   published. There is no way to look them up.**
3. **Bylaws and California nonprofit corporate law**, which vest management in the board and
   permit delegation. This sets who *may* be delegated authority, not the number.

**The one question that replaces all of this desk research**, to be asked in the first call,
before any price is named:

> *"If you decided you wanted this, what's the largest amount you can sign yourself — and does
> the answer change depending on which pot of money it comes out of?"*

That single question is worth more than everything in §A.3, because it also surfaces the
*funding source*, which is what §4 turns out to hinge on. It is not a rude question; procurement
staff answer it constantly and are usually relieved to be asked early.

**The design conclusion.** Three offers, landing at rungs 1, 2 and 6. Rung 1 is the one that is
signable by anybody. Rung 2 is the one that federal grant money can pay for without a quote.

the most plausible buyer. Nothing is priced between rungs, because a price between rungs buys
friction without buying revenue.

# B. THE COST FLOOR

## B.1 Rick's rate, stated explicitly

| Rate | Amount | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| **Published rate card rate** | **$100 / hour · $800 / day** | The rate that appears on every quote and every scope of work in this lane |
| **Internal opportunity-cost valuation** | **$1,000 / day** | What the corridor pilot brief uses to value founder time (`econ-dev-corridor-pilot-2026-09.md` §6.3). Used here to test whether an offer is genuinely worth doing, not to price it |
| **Absolute floor** | **$0 / day** | Cash-only cost. Shown so the true bottom is visible, and so it is obvious when Rick is donating labour rather than discounting |

**Why $100/hour is the published rate, and why that is not arbitrary.** CalOSBA's TAP program
announcement states: *"CalOSBA will reimburse consultant and staff time at a rate of no more than
$100 per hour except in very rare cases. These rates must be pre-approved by CalOSBA and include
a strong justification outlining why the Center is unable to procure a consultant at the rate of
$100 per hour or less. CalOSBA may request quotes from alternative consultants as part of this
evaluation."*
([TAP 2025-2026 Program Announcement](https://calosba.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/TAP-2025-2026_Program-Announcement.pdf),
Funding Categories §3, accessed 2026-09-23 — **verified verbatim**.)

So $100/hour is not a number Rick chose. It is **the ceiling the largest relevant funder in this
market has already published**. Pricing at exactly that ceiling means the rate can never be the
reason a TAP-funded buyer says no, and it means Rick never has to write the justification memo.
It is also a very good sentence to say out loud: *"My rate is $100 an hour, which is CalOSBA's
own cap — so it's pre-approved by construction."*

**Caveat, stated because it will bite otherwise.** That language is from the **FY2025-26**
announcement. The FY2026-27 announcement's full terms were not located as a retrievable
document on 2026-09-23; `california-econ-dev-grants-2026-09.md` already flags that the TAP Data
Model and counting rules may have changed for 2026-27. **Re-verify the rate cap against the
current year's announcement or a grantee's executed agreement before relying on it in a
meeting.**

## B.5 A correction to the corridor pilot's own ask

`econ-dev-corridor-pilot-2026-09.md` §8.3 says the ask after the pilot should be:

> *"A paid 90-day pilot: $12–18K, 60–100 businesses across two corridors."*

**At the rate card in §B.1, that scope is below cost.** 80 conversations at 5/day is 16 days of
conversations alone; add setup, two crosswalks, coding, two corridors' worth of report
production and delivery and it is roughly 30–35 working days, or **$24,000–$28,000 of founder
time before any cash cost**. Offering it at $12–18K is a 35–50% subsidy, and at the low end it is
close to half price.

That may still be the right call as a deliberate first-customer investment — but it should be a
decision with a number attached, not an estimate that happened to be optimistic. **Recommendation:
either reprice that ask to Offer C (§3.3), or cut the scope to 25–30 conversations on one
corridor and quote Offer B (§3.2).** Flagging this here rather than editing the campaign brief,
because the brief is a dated artifact and this is a pricing finding.

Three offers. Each is designed against a named rung from §A.5. Each states what the buyer gets,

### D.3.1 The finding, stated plainly

**Across every major program in this market, the administrative bucket is 10–20%, it is already
spent on salaries, and it frequently excludes software by name.** CalOSBA TAP is the clearest
case and also the most important one, because TAP grantees *are* the Tier 1 target list:

- A technology tool charged to Program Administration is capped at **$1,000**.
- Research costs "may not include the cost of research tools, software, or reports."
- Indirect and overhead are unallowable outright.
- But **contractor time to conduct monitoring and evaluation is expressly allowable**, at up to
  $100/hour.

**Therefore: UpSight cannot be sold into this market as a software subscription.** Not because
software is the wrong product — because the money cannot buy it. The offer must be structured,
scoped, invoiced and described as **a service: contractor time for monitoring, evaluation and
engagement documentation**, with the platform included as the means of delivery rather than as
the thing being bought.

Three practical consequences:

1. **The scope of work says "monitoring and evaluation services" and "engagement documentation,"
   not "platform subscription" or "licence."** Use the funder's own vocabulary. For a TAP
   grantee, the phrase "staff or contractor time to conduct monitoring and evaluation" is
   lifted verbatim from their own program announcement.
2. **The invoice shows hours and a rate**, because a cost-reimbursement grantee has to attach it
   to a reimbursement request and their program officer has to recognise it. An invoice that
   says "annual licence" is an invoice that gets questioned.
3. **If a software line item is genuinely needed** — because the client wants named-user access
   in their own name — put it at **$1,000 or less** under Program Administration as a
   pre-approved technology tool, or put it under **Marketing**, where database and technology
   subscriptions are explicitly allowable by name. Both routes are in the TAP text. This is
   worth knowing and worth saying, because it will read to a grants manager as someone who has
   actually read their program announcement, which almost no vendor has.

**This is the single most useful thing the research turned up**, and it is upstream of price.

strongest thing in this entire pricing exercise, and it is quoted from the buyer's own governing

# F. HOW TO QUOTE IT OUT LOUD

## F.1 The first deal

**When they ask "what does this cost?" — before any number, one question back:**

> *"Depends on one thing — which reporting obligation do you want me to take off your desk
> first? Give me that and I'll give you a number in the meeting."*

Then, having heard it:

> *"Nine thousand five hundred dollars. That's one quarter, one filing, fifteen of your
> businesses. You get the fields filled, the narrative written, and every number linked back to
> a dated conversation with a named business — so when your program officer asks where a figure
> came from, you click it.
>
> I picked that number deliberately. It's under every purchasing threshold I could find in this
> county, which means you can sign it yourself. No quotes, no RFP, no board item.
>
> What it costs you in staff time: two meetings and one introduction email. That's the whole
> list."*

**Then stop talking.** The corridor pilot brief is right that the silence does the work.

## F.2 The renewal

At the delivery meeting for the second filing, not by email:

> *"The annual version is forty-eight thousand — four cycles, up to a hundred businesses,
> quarterly, invoiced quarterly so it lines up with your own reimbursement cycle.
>
> Here's the part that's actually different from what you just bought. This year I asked your
> businesses twenty questions. Next year I ask them four, because I already know the other
> sixteen answers — and the report can show what *changed*, per business, with both quotes side
> by side. Nobody who starts next year can do that, including me, if we stop."*

And only then, if they lean in:

> *"The other thing that shows up in year two is the lease dates. Half a dozen of these come up
> in the same two quarters. That's not something I'd have sold you last year, because I couldn't
> have shown you anything."*

## F.3 "That's too expensive"

Do not discount. **Cut scope, visibly, at the published rate.**

> *"Then let's make it smaller rather than cheaper. The rate is a hundred an hour either way —
> that's CalOSBA's own cap, so it's pre-approved by construction. At nine-five you get fifteen
> conversations. What would ten get you? Because I'd rather do a smaller piece properly than
> discount this one and have us both pretend the scope didn't change."*

If they still balk, the real objection is almost never price at these numbers. It is one of three
things, and the right move is to name them:

> *"Can I ask — is it actually the money, or is it that you'd have to explain buying something
> from a one-person company? Because those are different problems and I've got a better answer
> for the second one."*

## F.4 "That seems suspiciously cheap"

This will happen, and it is the more dangerous of the two objections, because a cheap price in a
market anchored on $120,000 studies reads as *unserious* rather than *good value*.

**The answer is never "I'm new" or "I'm a startup." It is the cost structure.**

> *"It's cheap because the expensive part of a study is the part I don't do. A firm doing this
> mobilises a team, builds a research design from scratch, and rebuilds the baseline every time
> because the last study's data went into a PDF. I'm one person with a system that already knows
> your businesses, and the second year costs me less to deliver than the first.
>
> The number is what the work costs plus a margin. I'll show you the hours if you want them —
> they're on the invoice anyway, because that's how you'll need to expense it."*

**And then the constraint, which is the credibility move:**

> *"I'll tell you what you're not getting for that money. You're not getting peer-region
> benchmarking, you're not getting a facilitated board session, and you're not getting a name
> your council already trusts. If you need those, you need a firm, and I'll tell you which ones
> are good. What you're getting is the other fifty weeks."*

*Source: scripts authored for this document, 2026-09-23. **Not tested on any buyer.** The
"declining to oversell" move is adapted from `econ-dev-positioning-2026-09.md` §5.3.*

# G. THE TRAPS OF LOW PRICING, AND THE MECHANICS THAT PREVENT THEM

Four failure modes, each with a mechanism rather than a warning.

## G.1 Anchoring so low he can never raise it

**Mechanic: a published rate card with a named, dated, expiring discount.**

Every quote carries this block, on the quote, not in a follow-up:

> **Standard rate: $100/hour · $800/day.** *(This is CalOSBA's published reimbursement ceiling
> for consultant and staff time.)*
> **Filing Pilot, standard scope (15 conversations, one cycle): 8.5 days = $6,800 + $2,700
> production and platform = $9,500.**
> **Founding Client discount: 30% off the Annual Subscription for the first two agreements
> signed before 30 June 2027. After that, rate card.**

The discount is on the **subscription**, not on the pilot, and it is **quantity-limited and
date-limited**. That way the pilot price is the real price (so it never has to rise), and the
concession is a visible, expiring thing rather than a permanent position. The `pricing-strategy.md`
Founding Member offer uses the same structure for the SaaS motion; this is the same method with
different numbers, which is the point.

**What not to do:** never quote a round number with no basis. "$9,500" with an hours breakdown
behind it is a price. "$10,000, but for you $7,500" is the beginning of a negotiation Rick loses.

## G.2 Attracting buyers who do not value it

The corridor pilot brief §7 already names the signal — *"universal enthusiasm, zero budget
lines."* Cheap makes it worse, because it lowers the bar for saying yes without lowering the bar
for caring.

**Mechanic: a qualification gate the price does not open.** Offer A is not quoted until three
things are true, and they are asked in the first call:

1. **A named reporting obligation with a date.** Not "we'd love better data." A filing, a funder,
   a deadline.
2. **A named person who signs**, and the amount they can sign. (The §A.4 question.)
3. **A list.** They have contact information for at least 30 businesses they can introduce Rick
   to. **If they cannot produce a list, there is no engagement**, because the throughput
   assumption in §B.2 collapses and Offer A goes from 24% margin to a loss.

If any of the three is missing, the correct move is not a lower price. It is *"let's talk again
when you know which filing this is for."* That sentence costs nothing and it is the only
protection against the enthusiasm trap.

## G.3 A first deal that becomes the precedent every later buyer references

This is the real cost of being the low bidder in a small, dense, highly-connected market where
Tier 1 orgs sit on each other's boards and attend each other's events — which
`econ-dev-san-diego-target-list-2026-09.md` establishes in detail. **The first price will be
repeated in a room Rick is not in.**

**Three mechanics:**

1. **Make the pilot price the *published* price, not a favour.** A published $9,500 pilot that
   every subsequent buyer also pays is not a precedent problem — it is a product. A one-off
   $9,500 for a friend is a precedent problem forever.
2. **Never discount the pilot. Discount the subscription, once, publicly, with an expiry.**
   The number that will travel is the entry number; keep it clean.
3. **Write the case study around the *obligation*, not the price.** "Filed their quarterly TAP
   report with every number sourced, in eight weeks" travels. "Got it for nine and a half"
   travels too — and the only defence is that nine and a half is what it says on the website.

## G.4 Scope creep on a fixed price

The most likely way a 24%-margin engagement becomes a 0%-margin engagement.

**Four mechanics, all written into the agreement:**

| Boundary | The number | What happens past it |
|---|---|---|
| **Conversations** | 15 / 25 / 100 by offer | **$275 per additional conversation**, quoted and authorised in writing before it happens. (Cost basis: 0.2 days at $800 = $160. Margin 42%.) |
| **Geography** | One corridor, district or service area, named in the SOW | A second geography is a second engagement |
| **Obligations** | One reporting obligation for A; one filing period for B; four cycles against one obligation for C | A second funder's schema is a second crosswalk and is priced as such |
| **Revisions** | One round on the draft | Further rounds at the day rate, in half-day increments |
| **Meetings** | One kickoff, one delivery | Additional meetings at the day rate. **This is the one that quietly eats a solo founder** — a standing biweekly check-in is 12 hours a quarter that nobody priced |

**And one boundary that is not about money.** From `econ-dev-data-rights-2026-09.md`: bulk export
of recordings and transcripts (Layer 1) is **not** in any offer at any price, and no amount of
scope negotiation changes it. It is a consent commitment to the businesses, not a paid feature.
Say so early, in writing, next to the long list of things they *do* get — data-rights §4.5 is
explicit that the sequence matters.

## G.5 The trap the low-price position creates for the *positioning*

Worth naming because it is the one nobody writes down. `econ-dev-positioning-2026-09.md` §1 says
the category is "decision support," deliberately chosen to *"set the price anchor next to
consultants and data subscriptions, which is where it belongs."* A $9,500 entry price pulls
against that.

**The resolution is that the entry price is a *pilot*, and pilots are cheap in every category.**
A $9,500 pilot leading to a $48,000 annual subscription is coherent with a consulting anchor. A
$9,500 *product* is not. **So the word "pilot" must appear every single time the entry price
does**, and the subscription price must be quoted in the same conversation — not saved for later.
If a buyer ever hears $9,500 without hearing $48,000, the anchor is set at the wrong number and
it does not come back.
