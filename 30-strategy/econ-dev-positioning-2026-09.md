## 1. Position statement

Economic development organizations make expensive, public, hard-to-reverse decisions —
where to put incentive dollars, which corridor to prioritize, which employers are at risk,
what to tell a city council on Tuesday — on evidence that is thinner than anyone outside
the field would believe. The data they buy is regional and six to twenty-four months old.
The studies they commission are three years apart and start over each time. What the
businesses on the block actually said lives in a staff member's notebook until that staff
member leaves. **UpSight is decision support for economic development: a system where every
business conversation your staff already has becomes durable, attributable evidence — so
the grant report you owe your funder writes itself from work you are already doing, and by
year two you can see your corridor changing before the published data shows it.** The
report is why you buy it. The corpus is why you keep it.

**Category:** decision support for economic development.
Not "customer intelligence" (they have no customers in that sense, and the phrase invites
the wrong comparisons). Not "survey software" (that puts us in a $400/year category next to
tools they already have and do not respect). Decision support names the job and sets the
price anchor next to consultants and data subscriptions, which is where it belongs.

## 2. Who buys, who uses, who blocks, who is the audience

In a small company the buyer and the user are frequently the same person. In a government
or quasi-government organization they are almost never the same person, and there are two
more roles that do not exist in our normal ICP: a **blocker** who can stop the purchase for
reasons unrelated to value, and an **audience** who never touches the product but is the
reason it gets bought. Treat these as four distinct jobs.

| Role | Typical titles | What they care about | What they can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Economic buyer** | EDC President/CEO or Executive Director; City/County Economic Development Director or Manager; Chamber President/CEO; Main Street or BID Executive Director | Board and council credibility, grant compliance risk, staff capacity, "can I defend this line item" | Approves within their authority; sponsors it to a board or council above it |
| **User (the correspondent)** | Business Retention & Expansion Manager; Business Development Specialist; Corridor/District/Neighborhood Manager; Main Street program manager; SBDC advisor; outreach coordinator; frequently part-time, grant-funded, or a fellow/VISTA | Not re-typing the same notes; not losing what they learned; looking prepared on the next visit; not spending three weeks assembling the annual report | Uses it daily or not at all. Can kill it silently by not filing. Rarely can buy it |
| **Blocker** | Grants/Finance Manager, City Attorney, Records/Public-Records Officer, IT/InfoSec, Purchasing Agent | Allowable cost, records retention, public-records exposure, security review, procurement path, vendor viability | Can stop a decided purchase for months on grounds that have nothing to do with the product |
| **Audience** | City Council, County Board, EDC/Chamber board, state or federal program officer (EDA regional rep, CDBG monitor), assessed BID businesses, member businesses | "Is this true, and can you show me who said it" | Never logs in. Determines what the buyer needs the product to produce |

*Source: asserted by Rick Moy, 2026-09-23, from the structure of these organizations. Not
derived from interviews. **Highest-value thing the corridor pilot can do is correct this
table.***

**The consequence for how we sell.** The user has the pain and no budget. The buyer has the
budget and does not feel the daily pain — they feel the report deadline and the board
meeting. So the pitch is built for the buyer around an obligation (Section 5) while the
product is built for the user around field friction. If we pitch the user's pain to the
buyer, we sound like a productivity tool and get a "nice to have." If we build for the
buyer and ignore the user, nothing gets filed and the corpus never forms.

**The blocker is a first-class sales step, not an afterthought.** In this lane, "yes" from
the buyer is the middle of the sale, not the end. Plan the procurement, records and security
conversations into the sequence deliberately (Section 9).

## 3. The decisions we support, named

"Decision support" is meaningless until you can name the decisions. These are the ones
where the evidence is thinnest and the consequences are most public.

1. **Where the incentive dollars go.** Which corridor, which façade or tenant-improvement
   program, which microgrant round, which block gets the streetscape money.
2. **Who to visit next, and who is at risk.** Business retention triage: which employers
   are quietly considering relocation, contraction, or closure, and which are ready to
   expand and need something small to do it.
3. **Whether a program is working.** Renew, resize, or end the façade grant, the shop-local
   campaign, the technical assistance contract.
4. **What goes in the strategic plan.** The CEDS (Comprehensive Economic Development
   Strategy) or equivalent local strategy and its annual update, including the
   public-engagement documentation such a plan requires.
5. **What to say to the council in seven minutes.** The agenda item, the staff report, the
   answer to "how do you know that?" from the dais.
6. **Whether to back a specific physical change.** A rezoning, a road diet, a parking
   change, an eighteen-month construction detour — and what the businesses on that block
   actually said about it, on the record, with dates.
7. **Workforce alignment.** Which skills gaps employers name in their own words, against
   what the labor-market data implies.
8. **Disruption response.** After a fire, a flood, a plant closure, a major tenant leaving:
   who needs what, right now, and who is next.
9. **The grant application itself.** The need narrative, the documented engagement, the
   beneficiary evidence.
10. **The assessment or membership renewal.** For BIDs and chambers: demonstrating value to
    the businesses who pay for the organization to exist.

Note that #9 and #4 are the two that carry deadlines and money attached. That is not a
coincidence and it is the basis of Section 5.

## 4. The problem today

**How these decisions actually get made.** Asserted, not observed:

- **A person's memory and a notebook.** The corridor manager knows which businesses are
  struggling because she talks to them. That knowledge is not written down in any form that
  survives her. Public-sector and nonprofit EDO turnover is high; when she leaves, the
  relationships and the institutional read on the corridor leave with her, and her
  replacement starts from zero with businesses who now have to be re-earned.
- **An annual survey with a low response rate.** Sent to a list, answered by the businesses
  already engaged enough to answer surveys — which are the businesses least likely to be
  the ones in trouble. Same baseline questions every year, because the tool has no memory
  of last year's answers.
- **Purchased regional data.** Lightcast, Chmura JobsEQ, IMPLAN, ESRI, increasingly
  Placer.ai. Genuinely good at what it does (see Section 7). Structurally lagged, because
  it is built on administrative series that are published months after the quarter they
  describe, and structurally regional, because it is aggregated to geographies and industry
  codes that do not describe a two-block corridor.
- **A consultant study every three to five years.** Useful, credible, expensive, and a
  snapshot. It starts by re-establishing a baseline that the last study already established.
- **The annual report scramble.** Two to three weeks of reconstructing a year of activity
  from calendar entries, emails, and memory, so that a narrative and a set of counts can be
  filed with a funder.

**What it costs them.** Asserted:

| Cost | Mechanism | Real or illustrative |
|---|---|---|
| Staff weeks per reporting cycle | Reconstructing activity that was never captured at the time | Illustrative — magnitude unverified |
| Credibility at the dais | "How do you know that?" answered with an anecdote | Illustrative |
| Preventable closures | A business needed a small, cheap intervention and nobody knew in time | Illustrative — and the hardest thing in this whole document to ever prove |
| Rediscovery spend | Paying, every cycle, to re-learn what the organization already learned once | Illustrative |
| Turnover loss | Corridor knowledge held in one person's head, gone on their last day | Asserted from staffing structure |

*Source: asserted by Rick Moy, 2026-09-23. No EDO has told us any of this. Every row is a
hypothesis the pilot should either substantiate with a number or delete.*

**The honest version of the problem statement:** these organizations are not incompetent and
they are not under-informed about the region. They are under-informed about the **block**,
and everything they know about the block is stored in people rather than in systems.

## 5. The sequence: grant reporting is the wedge, early warning is the retention story

### 5.1 The order

**Wedge — grant reporting and compliance documentation.** Every pitch leads here. The
opening offer is: the engagement documentation, activity counts, and narrative evidence your
funder requires, produced from the fieldwork your staff is already doing, with every claim
traceable to a dated conversation with a named business.

**Retention — early warning.** Seeing distress or opportunity in a corridor before the
published data shows it. This is why they renew, why they add corridors, and why the corpus
is worth compounding. It is introduced after the first reporting cycle lands, using their
own accumulated data as the proof.

### 5.2 Why this order is right — do not reverse it

This section exists because the temptation to reverse it is strong and predictable. Early
warning is the more exciting story, it is the one that gets a nod in a conversation, and it
is the one a founder wants to lead with. Lead with it and the lane fails. Five reasons:

1. **The wedge is attached to money that already exists.** Grant administration and program
   delivery are funded, budgeted activities with an owner and a deadline. Early warning is
   not a line item in any economic development budget. Selling it means creating a new
   budget line in an organization that takes a year to create budget lines — or worse,
   asking a director to spend discretionary money on a hunch.
2. **The wedge is mandatory; early warning is elective.** Compliance failure has
   consequences — findings, clawback exposure, trouble on the next application. Nobody gets
   in trouble for not having early warning. Mandatory beats valuable in government buying,
   every time.
3. **Early warning is unprovable at the moment of purchase, and we have no track record.**
   You cannot demo a prediction. Asked "show me," we would have to say "give it eighteen
   months." That is a fine thing to say to an existing customer with a filled corpus and a
   catastrophic thing to say to a prospect. Worse: if we promise detection and the first
   closure we miss is a prominent one, we lose the account and the reference.
4. **Early warning invites a fight we lose today.** Lead with "we see the corridor changing"
   and a director with a Lightcast or Chmura subscription hears a claim against a vendor
   they already trust for indicators, on that vendor's turf, from a one-person company.
   Lead with documentation and there is no incumbent in the room, because nobody sells
   them that.
5. **The wedge produces the asset the retention story needs.** This is the part that makes
   the order not merely tactical but structural: the compliance fieldwork *is* the data
   collection. The visits they must document are the conversations that fill the corpus. We
   are not asking them to do new work so that we can later show them something; we are
   capturing work they are already obligated to do, and the by-product is the thing that
   becomes early warning. Reversing the order asks them to do the work *first*, on faith,
   for a payoff we cannot yet demonstrate.

**The rule, stated so it survives pressure:** *lead with the obligation, retain on the
insight.* Or, for a deck: **file the report, keep the intelligence.**

### 5.3 What this means for the pitch

The first call never opens with the corridor-intelligence story. It opens with: *what do
you have to report, to whom, by when, and how do you assemble it today?* Early warning
enters the conversation only if the prospect raises it — and when they do, the correct
answer is "that's year two, and it will come out of the same fieldwork; let's get the
reporting right first." Saying that out loud is itself a credibility move: a vendor
declining to oversell the exciting thing is unusual enough to be memorable.

## 6. The three-part differentiation

### 6.1 Compounding intelligence

A consultant's study is a snapshot that begins decaying the day it is delivered. A survey
resets to zero every time it is fielded. UpSight's corpus is the opposite shape: every
conversation makes the next question better and makes every prior conversation more
valuable, because a second data point on the same business turns a description into a
trajectory.

**Why it is hard to copy.** Not because the software is hard. Because:

- **You cannot backfill a baseline.** A competitor entering a corridor in year three cannot
  manufacture year one. Longitudinal same-business data is only available to whoever was
  asking two years ago.
- **Respondent burden falls as value rises.** A survey re-asks its baseline every wave, so
  it gets more tedious for the respondent each time. A system that knows what a business
  said last year opens with "last spring you said the parking change was your biggest
  worry — where did that land?" and asks four questions instead of twenty. Response quality
  goes up while burden goes down. That is the inverse of survey fatigue, and it compounds.
- **The switching cost is the corpus, not the contract.** A study is a deliverable you own
  and can take anywhere. A corpus is an accumulated position you would have to rebuild.

**What it means economically to the buyer.** They stop paying repeatedly to rediscover the
same things. Today, each study, each survey wave, and each new staff member re-establishes
a baseline that the organization already paid to establish. Call it the **rediscovery tax**.
Under a compounding model, year two costs less per unit of evidence than year one, and year
three less again, because the baseline is already there and the marginal conversation is
short. This is the argument that lets us stand next to a consulting line item rather than a
software one.

**The honest limits.**

- **The corpus decays if the cadence stops.** Businesses close, owners sell, contacts leave.
  A corpus that is not refreshed becomes a historical document within about two years.
  Compounding is a consequence of sustained fieldwork, not a property of the software.
- **The moat protects the buyer more than it protects us.** The corpus belongs to the EDO.
  That is the right and defensible commitment to make (Section 9), and it means our lock-in
  is weaker than the phrase "compounding moat" implies. Our retention comes from being the
  place the work happens, not from holding data hostage. Say this plainly; do not let the
  deck imply otherwise.

### 6.2 Evidence-linked receipts

Every insight stays attached to the verbatim quote that produced it, with the business, the
date, and the person. This transfers directly from the core UpSight positioning, and it is
worth more here than it is in our normal market, for two reasons specific to government:

- **The audience is adversarial by design.** A council member's job includes asking "how do
  you know that?" A staff report that can answer "forty-one businesses on that corridor
  between March and August; here are the eleven who raised it, here is what they said" is a
  categorically different artifact from one that says "we've heard from businesses that."
- **Funders audit.** Documented engagement is not a nice-to-have in a grant report; it is
  the thing being verified. Traceability is compliance infrastructure here, not a trust
  feature.

The corollary is a discipline we must hold: **an AI-generated summary that cannot show its
receipts is worse than useless in front of a council — it is a liability.** Every
council-facing or funder-facing artifact shows sources on the artifact itself.

### 6.3 Correspondent presence

**The frame.** The person in the field is not a researcher running a study and not a
consultant parachuting in for an engagement. They are a **correspondent**: someone with a
beat, continuously present, on first-name terms with the businesses on it, filing regularly.

**What a correspondent actually does:**

- **Holds a beat.** A corridor, a district, or an industry cluster — a defined territory
  they are responsible for knowing.
- **Is present between events.** They are there when nothing is happening, which is why they
  are trusted when something is.
- **Files short and often.** Not an annual report. A filing after each visit — what changed,
  who said what, what to watch. The unit is the visit, not the study.
- **Builds sources over time.** The fifth conversation with an owner is worth more than the
  first, and the correspondent is judged on the beat, not on any single filing.

**Why the frame earns trust from businesses.** A business owner will ignore a survey from
the city and will talk to a person who comes by. The relationship is the instrument. The
frame also disclaims the three roles a small business most fears from a government-adjacent
visitor: the correspondent is not an inspector, not a salesperson, and not code enforcement.
For immigrant-owned, cash-heavy, or lease-insecure businesses, that disclaimer is the whole
ballgame — and it has to be true, not just said.

**Why the frame earns trust from agencies.** It is legible. "Correspondent" maps to a job
they can fund (outreach specialist, BR&E coordinator, district manager) and to a deliverable
they can report (visits made, businesses contacted, engagement documented). It also carries
connotations of accuracy and independence, which is what a director wants to be able to tell
a board about where their information comes from.

**What it implies about cadence and staffing.**

| Parameter | Working assumption | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Active relationships per correspondent | 80–150 businesses | Illustrative. Assumed, not measured |
| Touch cycle | Every 6–8 weeks for active accounts; annual for the long tail | Illustrative |
| Field days | 4–6 substantive visits per field day | Illustrative |
| Time to file | Within 24 hours, or it is lost | Asserted as a design constraint |
| Staffing shape | Often part-time, contract, or grant-funded; bilingual where the corridor requires it | Asserted from how these roles are funded |

*Source: asserted by Rick Moy, 2026-09-23, as design assumptions for the corridor pilot. No
observed EDO staffing data behind any row. These numbers exist to be replaced.*

The product consequence is sharp: **filing must take five minutes standing on a sidewalk.**
Voice capture, mobile, the business's profile already loaded before the visit with what they
said last time. If filing takes twenty minutes at a desk, it will not happen, and the entire
position collapses to an empty database.

**Where the metaphor breaks down — honestly:**

1. **Loyalty.** A journalist's obligation runs to the public and they publish what they
   learn. A correspondent working for an EDC works for the agency, and much of what they
   hear is told in confidence — a business quietly planning layoffs is not filing a news
   story. Mishandling that destroys the relationship and can create real legal exposure
   (Section 9, public records). The frame must never imply publication.
2. **Neutrality.** Correspondents observe; these staff are expected to *help* — connect a
   business to a loan program, fix a permit problem. That is good for access and terrible
   for neutrality: the person collecting the information is also the person whose program is
   being evaluated. Expect favorable bias in what businesses say about the EDO itself.
3. **Representativeness.** Journalism has verification norms but does not claim sampling
   validity, and neither can we. A correspondent program produces **systematic qualitative
   coverage of a defined territory** — not a representative sample with a margin of error.
   Anyone who presents corridor findings to a council as statistically representative is
   misusing it, and our artifacts should make the distinction visible on the page.
4. **The audience.** "Filing" implies readers. Most of this is internal, read by two people.
   The ritual of filing is what we want from the metaphor; the publication is not.

## 8. Message pyramid

### Headline

> **File the report. Keep the intelligence.**

Plain-English subhead for a buyer who has never heard of us:

> *The business visits your staff already makes become documented engagement your funder
> accepts — and a record of your corridor that gets more useful every year instead of
> starting over.*

### Supporting claim 1 — Your reporting obligation gets easier and more defensible

*Proof required:*
- A generated engagement narrative and activity summary where every claim links to a dated,
  named conversation, exported in a format a program officer accepts.
- Counts a funder asks for — businesses contacted, by date, by geography — produced without
  reconstruction.
- A before/after on staff hours to produce one reporting cycle, measured at a real EDO.
- **Status: not yet proven. Requires one pilot to complete one full reporting cycle.**

### Supporting claim 2 — Nothing you learn resets

*Proof required:*
- A single business profile showing three years of conversations in one place.
- A year-two interview that opens with what the business said in year one, and is visibly
  shorter as a result.
- A demonstrated "we already asked this" retrieval in front of a prospect, using their data.
- **Status: partially provable today.** The mechanism exists in the product. What is unproven
  is that it holds up on EDO-shaped data over EDO-shaped time.

### Supporting claim 3 — You see the corridor change before the published data does

*Proof required:*
- A documented instance where the corpus surfaced a change some number of months before it
  appeared in published series or in an actual closure — dated, side by side, with the
  original quotes.
- Enough such instances to distinguish signal from a lucky anecdote.
- **Status: not proven, not claimable, and not to be used in an opening pitch.** Until we
  have at least one dated, verifiable instance from a pilot, this claim appears in internal
  strategy documents only. See Section 5.2.

## 9. Objections and honest answers

**"Why would we trust a government contract to a one-person company?"**
You should not bet a five-year program on a solo vendor. Bet a twelve-month pilot on one,
with your data portable from day one. Concretely: export of the full corpus in open formats
on demand and on termination, written into the agreement; a named continuity and
data-egress commitment; a pilot scoped small enough that failure costs you a season, not a
strategy. The honest counterweight is that a solo vendor gives you the founder on the phone
rather than a support queue — but that is a reason to pilot, not a reason to standardize.
*Do not oversell this. A director who is worried about vendor viability is being competent.*

**"How would this even get through procurement?"**
Three known paths, to be confirmed per jurisdiction: (a) keep the pilot under the applicable
micro-purchase threshold so competitive quotes are not required — for federal award funds
the federal micro-purchase threshold is $10,000 and the simplified acquisition threshold is
$250,000 under the OMB Uniform Guidance at 2 CFR 200 **(published figures, and they have
been revised recently — verify current values and the entity's own lower local threshold
before quoting them to anyone)**; (b) a cooperative purchasing vehicle; (c) contract with
the nonprofit EDC, chamber, or Main Street organization rather than the city, where that
entity exists and is not bound by municipal purchasing rules. Path (c) is frequently the
fastest and is a reason to target independent EDCs and chambers before city departments.

**"What happens to what businesses tell us under a public records request?"**
This is the sharpest objection in the lane and we should not pretend it is solved. If the
record sits in a government system it may be disclosable under the state public records act.
A business owner telling a corridor manager they may lay off twenty people does not expect
to read it in the paper. Honest answer: (1) the correspondent is explicit with every
business about what is on and off the record, and the product records that distinction at
capture time; (2) where an independent nonprofit EDC or chamber holds the corpus, the
exposure profile is different and usually better; (3) aggregation and redaction tiers exist
so that a council-facing artifact can carry a finding without carrying an attributable
quote; (4) trade-secret and competitive-harm exemptions exist in most state acts but are
narrow and we will not promise coverage. **This needs a jurisdiction-specific legal review
in the first pilot, and the answer belongs in the contract, not in the sales deck.**

**"We already do surveys."**
Keep them — we will ingest them. Two things a survey cannot do: it re-asks its baseline
every wave, and it measures the businesses willing to answer surveys, which are
systematically the ones already engaged and least likely to be the ones in trouble. The
businesses whose answers would change your decisions are the ones who do not answer.

**"Our consultant already does this."**
They probably do it better than we will in month one — they have benchmarks, peer-region
comparisons, and a name your board trusts. What they do not have is the other fifty weeks
of the year, and their next study will start by re-establishing the baseline their last one
established. We are the instrument between studies, and we make the next study cheaper
because the fieldwork is already done and attributable. *Where possible, meet the consultant
early and position as an input to their work. They are a better referral channel than
adversary.*

**"Shouldn't we just hire another staff person?"**
Yes, probably. The correspondent is a person, not software. We are what that person files
into, and the reason their knowledge outlives their tenure. If you have nobody in the field,
we cannot help you yet — and we will say so rather than sell you an empty database.

**"How do I know the AI isn't making things up in front of my council?"**
Every claim in a council-facing artifact links to a dated quote from a named business. The
artifact shows its sources on the page. If a statement has no receipt, it does not appear.
That constraint is the product, not a setting.

**"Our data can't leave our environment / we need a security review."**
Expect this and budget calendar time for it. It is a legitimate blocker we have not yet
solved for this lane, and we should know our answer on hosting, retention, access control,
and review documentation before the first serious conversation rather than during it.

## 10. What must be true for this positioning to hold

Ranked by how badly the lane breaks if the assumption is false. Every one of these is
currently untested.

| # | Assumption | If false | How to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Someone is actually in the field with enough regularity to form a corpus** — a correspondent exists at the organization, or can be funded | Everything collapses. Compounding, early warning, and receipts all rest on sustained field presence that software cannot supply | In the first ten conversations, ask: who visits businesses, how often, and is that role funded next year? |
| **2** | Grant and compliance reporting is painful enough to pay to fix — not merely annoying | The wedge fails and there is no other funded door | Ask what they report, to whom, how long assembly takes, and what happened the last time it was late |
| **3** | There is an existing budget line a subscription can attach to without new appropriation | Sales cycles stretch past our cash runway even where buyers want it | Ask the finance or grants manager directly where such a tool would be charged |
| **4** | Businesses will speak candidly to a government-adjacent correspondent | The corpus fills with pleasantries and the evidence is worthless | Compare what the same business says to the correspondent versus to an independent interviewer |
| **5** | The corpus produces a visible early-warning signal within 12–18 months | Retention story disappears; the product is a compliance tool, which is a smaller and more replaceable business | Instrument for it from day one: timestamp everything, and check findings against later published data and actual closures |
| **6** | A solo vendor can clear procurement, records, and security review | Wedge is sound but unreachable; requires a partner or reseller path | Attempt one real procurement early and deliberately, as a test rather than as a deal |
| **7** | Government sales cycles are survivable at our cash position | The lane is right and we cannot afford to run it now | Track time-to-first-dollar on the first three, honestly |
| **8** | These organizations can pay enough to matter — many are two-to-five-person shops | Right buyer, wrong economics; needs a multi-site, state-association, or per-corridor model | Price-test a pilot early rather than after positioning is fixed |

*Source: asserted by Rick Moy, 2026-09-23. No supporting evidence for any row.*

**The weakest assumption is #1**, and it deserves saying plainly: this entire position
depends on a person walking a corridor regularly and filing what they hear, and UpSight
cannot create that person. Software does not generate field presence. If an EDO has nobody
in the field, the corpus never forms, the compounding argument is theoretical, early warning
never arrives, and we have sold a well-designed empty database.

The tempting fix is the dangerous one: **supply the correspondent ourselves.** That turns
this into a services business with software attached, which is the same failure mode flagged
in `decision-files-gameplan.md` — "engagements stop converting to licenses → it became a
consultancy." If we ever staff correspondents, it should be a deliberate, priced, bounded
decision with its own entry in `00-control/decisions.md`, not a drift that happens one
accommodating pilot at a time.

## 11. Open questions for the corridor pilot

1. Who actually holds the pen on the annual grant report, and how many hours does it take?

## 12. What we do not say in this lane

- "Customer intelligence platform." Wrong category, wrong comparisons, wrong buyer.
