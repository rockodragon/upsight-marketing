# The Decision Files — contributor board charter

**Date:** 2026-08-15 · **Status:** proposed
**Why it exists:** access is easy, candor is scarce. A board of recognized former CISOs and analysts who *vouch and source* is what converts access into candor — and it's the moat a competitor with a good brand and a budget can't buy.

---

## What it does for the business

| Problem | What the board fixes |
|---|---|
| Weak moat at the start | Relationships and vouching aren't replicable. This is the scarce asset. |
| Rick's ~2.5 hrs/month caps throughput | Contributors conduct interviews. 10/month becomes 30 without hiring. |
| "Will a vendor pay a one-person shop?" | With a named masthead it isn't a one-person shop. Raises the price ceiling, not just the close rate. |
| No forced-participation dynamic (the NSS lever) | A category report backed by six known CISOs is one a vendor can't afford to be absent from. |
| Site has no masthead (outreach blocker) | Solved the week the first three say yes. |

## Call them contributors, not advisors

"Advisory board" is the word for people who lend a logo and never answer email. The
distinction that matters is whether there's a defined job. Suggested title:
**Contributing Analyst** — it says they do work, and it's the title that makes a
CISO answer *their* email too.

## The job — pick one tier per person

| Tier | Commitment | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Contributing Analyst** | ~4 hrs/quarter | Conducts 1 interview/quarter, makes 2 warm intros/quarter, reviews one file before publication. |
| **Sourcing Contributor** | ~1 hr/quarter | Intros only — 2–3 per quarter. Named on the masthead. |
| **Editorial Reviewer** | ~2 hrs/quarter | Reads files pre-publication for whether they'd survive a CISO's scrutiny. No sourcing. |

**Make the ask specific and small.** "Join my advisory board" gets a yes and then
nothing. "Two intros a quarter and one 20-minute interview" gets a yes and then the
thing actually happening.

## Compensation — pay them something

Free labor from friends works for about two months. Options, in order of preference
for a company at this stage:

1. **Small equity grant** (0.1–0.5%, 2-year vest with a 6-month cliff) for
   Contributing Analysts. Aligns them to the outcome and costs no cash.
2. **Per-file honorarium** ($500–1,500) for a conducted interview or a named
   contribution. Clean, cash, no cap-table complexity.
3. **Revenue share on sourced engagements** (10–15% of first-year engagement value)
   for anyone who introduces a paying vendor. This is the one that actually moves
   behavior — it turns the board into a channel.

Byline credit is real compensation too for this crowd, but it can't be the only one.

## Conflicts policy — the part that protects the whole business

Independence is the product. A board member on a vendor payroll destroys it faster
than any editorial mistake.

1. **Disclosed affiliations, published on the site.** Current employer, board seats,
   advisory roles, and any vendor equity. Updated at least annually.
2. **No one currently employed by, or on retainer to, a security vendor** may be a
   Contributing Analyst. Sourcing Contributor is acceptable **with disclosure.**
3. **Category recusal.** A contributor recuses from any file touching a category
   where they hold a disclosed interest. Recusals are noted in the file's
   methodology block, not handled quietly.
4. **The firewall extends to the board.** No contributor sees a paid-engagement
   deliverable. Ever.
5. **Sitting CISOs** are welcome as *participants*, and can be Sourcing Contributors,
   but their employer must be disclosed and they don't review files touching their
   own vendor stack.

If a rule here ever feels inconvenient, that's the rule doing its job.

## Editorial control stays with Rick

Contributors source, interview, and review. **Rick owns the published conclusion.**

A board that decides findings is a committee, and committees smooth the edges off
exactly the parts that make a file worth reading. Review means *"would this survive
a CISO's scrutiny"* — not *"do I agree with the take."* Say this explicitly in the
invitation; experienced people appreciate a clear line and will decline gracefully
if they wanted a different one.

## The recruiting ask

> [Name] — I'm reviving something close to what we did at NSS, aimed at how buyers
> actually decide instead of how products test.
>
> The Decision Files publishes one real decision at a time: the situation, what was
> at stake, how the person decided, and how it actually turned out. Independent, and
> the buyers stay anonymous when they need to.
>
> I want six people whose names mean something to be Contributing Analysts. The
> commitment is genuinely small — two intros a quarter and one 20-minute interview —
> and I'd rather have your judgment on the work than your logo on the page.
> [Compensation] and a byline on anything you contribute to.
>
> Worth 20 minutes to talk about it?

## Sequencing

1. **Get three yeses before publishing the masthead.** One name reads as a favor;
   three reads as an institution.
2. **The first three should be the most recognizable ones you can get** — the
   masthead's job is to make the fourth through tenth asks easy.
3. **Give the first contributor a real, small job inside two weeks** of them saying
   yes. A board that isn't used in month one is decorative by month three.
4. **Publish the conflicts policy at the same time as the masthead.** Doing it later
   looks like a response to something.

## What to watch

- **The decorative-drift signal:** a quarter passes and no contributor sourced an
  interview. Fix it with a smaller, more specific ask — not with more names.
- **The consensus signal:** files start reading smoother and less surprising. That's
  editorial control leaking. Pull it back.
- **The conflict signal:** a contributor advocates for a category or vendor in a way
  that doesn't track the evidence. Handle it immediately and visibly, or the
  independence claim becomes a claim.
