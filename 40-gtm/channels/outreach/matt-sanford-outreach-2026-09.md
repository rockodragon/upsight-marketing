## Part 1 — The outreach

### The three questions, and why these three

Each one is chosen because the answer changes the plan, not because it flatters. If all three
come back the way I expect, the wedge holds. If any one comes back differently, the lane needs
rebuilding before Rick spends another week on it.

1. **Who actually assembles the report** — the plan changes depending on whether the buyer is
   him, a program director, a part-time grant writer, or an outside consultant.
2. **What the failure mode is** — whether reports break on *numbers you can't source* or on
   *outcomes you know happened but can't evidence* determines whether UpSight is a data tool or
   an evidence tool. It is built to be the second. If the answer is the first, the wedge is wrong.
3. **Whether reporting capacity is a budget line** — determines whether this is a funded purchase
   or a favor. Grant-funded orgs can often charge reporting to the grant. If they can't, pricing
   has to change.

### The one ask, and why it's this one

**The ask is: 20 minutes on the phone in the next two weeks, with the Expo as an in-person
alternative.** That is one ask with two ways to say yes — not two asks.

Why this and nothing else:

- **It costs him almost nothing and costs him nothing politically.** He is six months into a new
  CEO seat. He is being asked for things constantly. Twenty minutes with no deliverable attached
  is the only request at this stage with a near-zero refusal cost.
- **It is the right size for the information being requested.** All three questions are about how
  his organization works. That is a conversation, not a form. Asking for a written answer to
  question 2 would get a polite non-answer.
- **The Expo option removes the calendar as an obstacle.** He is already going to be in a room on
  the 29th. "I'll come to you, standing up, twenty minutes" is harder to decline than a Calendly link.
- **"I'll send you what I learn either way" is the reciprocity, and it must be real.** Rick has to
  actually produce and send that. If he doesn't, this lane is dead in a small community.

**Asks deliberately NOT made first, and why:**

| Rejected ask | Why not first |
|---|---|
| Sponsorship of the corridor study | Far too large before he knows Rick. Sponsorship is Touch 3, minimum — see the letter below. |
| "Send me a grant report you filed" | Reads as a document request from a stranger. May be politically awkward even when the report is public. Ask on the call instead, once he's explained the process himself. |
| A ride-along on a business retention visit | Genuinely the highest-value thing Rick could get, and exactly the correspondent frame — but it commits staff time and implies Rick is inside the tent. Ask for it at the *end* of a good call, not in the cold email. |
| Product demo | There is no product story yet for this buyer. Demoing before the three questions are answered means demoing the wrong thing. |
| An intro to other EDC directors | Premature. He has no reason to spend his network yet. This becomes the real prize at Touch 2. |

### Sequencing — what happens on yes

**Touch 1 — the call (20 min).** Ask the three questions in order, then shut up. Two things to
listen for that he won't volunteer:
- *Who the real owner is.* If he names a person who isn't him, that person is the buyer and Rick
  needs a warm handoff to them by the end of the call.
- *Whether he describes a reporting cycle or a reporting crisis.* Cycle = a product sale.
  Crisis = a services engagement first, product later.

Close with exactly one escalation, chosen by how the call went:
- Call went well → **"Can I come with you on two business visits and write up what I hear?"**
  That is the ride-along, and it is the whole correspondent thesis in one request.
- Call went politely → **"Who else should I ask these three questions?"** Two names is a win.

**Touch 2 — within 5 business days.** Send the promised cross-organization write-up. Short, real,
useful on its own, no product mentioned. This is the trust deposit and the reason he takes the
next call.

**Touch 3 — 2 to 4 weeks after that.** The sponsorship letter below. Not before. It only makes
sense once he has heard Rick ask good questions and then deliver something unprompted.

**Retention story stays holstered.** Early warning — spotting a corridor going soft before the
lagging data shows it — does not appear in Touch 1 or Touch 2, and appears in the letter only as
the closing line about what the corpus becomes. It is why they renew in year two. It is not why
they say yes in week one. If Rick leads with it he sounds like a forecasting vendor, and the
funded, painful, recurring obligation — the report that is due — goes unmentioned.

I'd like ECEDC to sponsor a piece of field research in East County, and I want to be direct

actually happened to a business, and the only real proof is a conversation someone had in the
field months ago and wrote up from memory. This study produces that proof as a byproduct: twenty
businesses, on the record, with their words attached to the outcome. That is reporting evidence
you can cite, not a narrative you have to reconstruct.

**What you get.**
- A published report with ECEDC named as sponsor, and the underlying interview record — every
  business, every quote, source-linked — delivered to you whether or not it ends up in the report.
- Quarterly data you can put in front of the County, the EDA, and your board that nobody else in

- First look before publication. You see it before the press does.

**What the sponsorship does not buy** — and this is the point of it. You don't pick the businesses.
You don't see the questions in advance. You don't change a finding. If the corridor is in trouble,
the report says so. Research a sponsor can steer is worth nothing to the funder you'd hand it to,
which is the only reason it's worth sponsoring in the first place.

### Notes on the letter (not part of the letter)

- **Order is load-bearing.** Grant reporting appears in paragraph two and carries the money
  argument. Early warning appears in one clause near the end, explicitly labeled as year two.
  If a reviewer moves early warning up, the letter stops working: the funded obligation is what
  makes this a budget item rather than a nice idea.
- **The "what sponsorship does not buy" paragraph is the strongest paragraph in the letter** and
  is lifted directly from the Decision Files independence rules in
  `40-gtm/channels/outreach/decision-files-onepager.md`. Keep it. It converts a sponsor pitch
  into a credibility pitch, and it is the reason the output is usable as funder-facing evidence.
- **"Business twelve" is deliberate.** A specific-sounding number does more for the correspondent
  frame than the word "ongoing."
- **Do not send this cold.** It assumes Touch 1 and Touch 2 have happened. Sent cold to a
  six-month CEO it reads as a solicitation and burns the StartupSD connection.
- **Open items for Rick:** the sponsorship number; whether the 20-business corridor study has a
  defined timeline he can commit to in writing; and whether ECEDC's Foundation can actually take
  a sponsorship of this shape, which is a question for the call, not a thing to assume in the letter.
