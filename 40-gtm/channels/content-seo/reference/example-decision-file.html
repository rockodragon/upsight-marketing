How a 60-engineer team picked one AI coding tool — and what the pilot didn't tell them

They ran a structured six-week evaluation across four tools. The thing that decided it never appeared on the scorecard, and the thing they scored highest turned out not to matter at all.

Interview conducted 6 Aug 2026 · Subject: VP Engineering, 60 engineers, Series B fintech · Illustrative example — not a real interview

The 30-second version

Who
VP Engineering at a 60-person Series B fintech
The decision
Which AI coding tool to standardise on, after 18 months of everyone using whatever they liked
What was at stake
$180K/year in licences, plus the credibility of saying no to four teams
What they chose
One tool company-wide, with a documented exception process
What happened
Adoption held at 78% after five months. Two teams still run something else, officially.
The lesson
Benchmarks separated nobody. Whether it worked on their oldest codebase separated everybody.

The situation

We weren't trying to save money. We were trying to stop having the same argument in every architecture review. VP Engineering

By early 2026 the team was running four AI coding tools at once. Nobody had chosen them — they'd arrived one team at a time, mostly on individual credit cards, and finance had started asking questions about the aggregate.

The trigger wasn't cost. It was a security review that surfaced three tools with different data-handling terms, one of which nobody could produce a signed agreement for.

How they decided

The scorecard was written before any vendor call — five criteria, weighted, agreed by the VP Engineering, two staff engineers and the security lead. It is a better process than most teams run. It still didn't predict the outcome.

What they'd do differently

I'd give one engineer a day and our three ugliest repos before I'd run another six-week pilot. We'd have got to the same answer in week one. VP Engineering

The pilot ran on active, well-maintained services — which is where engineers naturally reach for a new tool, and exactly where every option performs well. The differentiating test was the one nobody scheduled.

What this one case can't tell you

One company, one codebase, one team's tolerance for being told what to use. We don't know whether the legacy-code test generalises, whether 78% adoption is good or bad against peers, or what the two holdout teams would say — we didn't interview them.

Decision principle

Pilot on your worst code, not your best. Every tool works on the code you'd be happy to show someone.

Analyst counterpoint

The obvious reading — that the scorecard was a waste — is too neat. The scorecard eliminated two of four options on cost and data terms, which is most of the work. What it failed at was separating the finalists, and that's a narrower and more common failure: weighted criteria are good at exclusion and bad at selection.

The stronger criticism is of the pilot design, not the scorecard. Six weeks on healthy services tested the wrong thing for six weeks. That was a planning miss, and it's the part most teams would repeat.
