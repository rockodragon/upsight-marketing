The Decision Files · visual system v4

Decision file visual patterns

One diagram per file. It should be readable in ten seconds, in plain language, before anyone reads the write-up. Eight patterns, matched to the kinds of decisions people actually make. Examples use illustrative data.

01 Decision timeline

Use for: decisions where the calendar tells the story

Vertical, so it holds fifteen rows as easily as five. Shows where the months went, and where the call actually got made.

Replacing a SIEM · 14 months

Built from the buyer's own calendar and purchase records. Illustrative.

The point: nine months of formal evaluation, and the winner was picked on a question nobody had written down.

02 Who dropped out, and why

Use for: any shortlist

People rationalise what they bought. They remember exactly why they threw something out.

Same decision · four vendors

Reasons are the buyer's own words from the interview. Illustrative.

Incumbent Too expensive Price went up 28% at our data volume, and the pricing model changed halfway through the renewal
Vendor B Nobody used it Our analysts stopped opening it two weeks into the trial
Vendor C No real support You only get a named engineer on the tier above what we could afford
Vendor D Chosen Weakest feature list of the three finalists. Best answer on who we call at 3am.

The point: two of the four were thrown out for reasons nobody had scored — a price change and whether the team would actually use it.

03 What we said mattered vs. what actually mattered

Use for: the default — any decision with a scorecard

Their scorecard on the left, what really happened on the right. The gap between the two is the whole story.

Their scorecard, and what each line actually did

Where the list came from: the security team wrote and weighted it themselves, before they spoke to any vendor. Not a vendor's list. Right-hand column: what actually happened with each item, in their words. Illustrative.

What we said we'd judge onWeightWhat actually happened
Threat detection coverage30% All four had it. Never came up again after the first demo.
Works with our existing tools20% All four had the twelve connections we needed.
Fast enough at our data volume15% We tested it twice. All four were fast enough.
Price25% Threw out the incumbent. The other three cost about the same.
Support when something breaks10% This is what we actually bought on. We gave it the lowest weight on the sheet.
Would the team actually use itnot on the list Threw out Vendor B. It never occurred to us to score this.

The point: 65% of the scorecard came down to things every vendor could do. They bought on a line worth 10%, and the two things that hurt most weren't on the sheet at all.

04 Feature matrix — with the truth on it

Use for: crowded categories where everyone looks the same on paper

The familiar grid, with three changes. Four levels instead of yes/no, because most "yes" answers are really "yes, with a caveat." A score per vendor so you can see who has more without counting. And the row that actually decided it, highlighted.

What each vendor could do · scored during trials

Scoring: full = 1 point, partial = ½, costs extra = ½, none = 0. Recorded by the buyer's team during hands-on trials, not from vendor datasheets. The highlighted row is the one they said made the call. Illustrative.

06 What we cut, what we kept

Use for: consolidating tools · merging two companies' stacks

The survivors are the interesting part. They show which tools are genuinely load-bearing versus which just have a defender.

Nine tools down to four · 11 months

From the buyer's own tool inventory, before and after. Illustrative.

Switched off — 4 tools Did the same job as something else, barely used, and all renewed in the same quarter
Folded into the platform — 3 tools Same job, done by the new system. Budget freed up.
Survived anyway — 2 tools One because a regulator names it. One because a single engineer's workflow depends on it and nobody wanted that argument.

The point: consolidation always stops in the same place. What survives isn't the best tool — it's the one with a regulator or a defender behind it.

07 Cost crossover

Use for: build vs. buy · in-house vs. outsourced

Where the two cost lines cross, and whether anyone got there. Most of these decisions rest on a crossover the company never reaches.

Building our own team vs. paying a provider · 5 years

The buyer's own model as shown to their board, with what actually happened laid over it. Illustrative.

The point: the decision rested on breaking even in year four, which depended on a hiring plan that never happened.

08 What we skipped

Use for: emergencies — breach, outage, deadline, vendor goes away

When there's no time, what they cut is more revealing than what they did.

Replacing endpoint security in 6 weeks after our vendor was acquired

Their normal buying process, against what six weeks allowed. Illustrative.

Formal tenderSkipped Went straight to two vendors already approved by procurement
TrialCut to 2 weeks Normally eight weeks across four business units. We ran one unit for two weeks.
Security reviewKept in full The one thing nobody was willing to shorten
Calling their customersSkipped This is the one that cost us. Two of three would have warned us about the conflict we hit in month two.

The point: the shortcut that hurt wasn't the shortened trial. It was the three phone calls nobody had time to make.

Language rule for every diagram: write what the buyer would say out loud. "Too expensive," not "eliminated on cost criteria." "Nobody used it," not "failed adoption threshold." If a label needs a glossary, it's the wrong label.
