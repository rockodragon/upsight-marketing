# Campaign — Self-funded corridor pilot: 20 businesses, one San Diego corridor

> **The whole design constraint: nothing in the critical path requires anyone else to say yes.**
> No procurement, no sponsor, no sanction, no MOU, no grant. Rick walks a corridor, talks to
> twenty businesses, and shows up to the agency conversation with a finished study instead of a
> proposal.
>
> **Governing order for this lane — do not reorder in any buyer-facing material:**
> **grant reporting is the WEDGE** (funded, recurring, mandatory). **Early warning is the
> RETENTION story** (why they renew, not why they buy). Differentiator is **compounding
> intelligence**. Field frame is the **correspondent**, not the consultant.

---

## Honesty note, up front

**This lane has zero customer evidence behind it.** There is not one interview, one quote, or one
survey response from an EDC, chamber, or city economic development office anywhere in this repo.
The wedge/retention ordering above is a *hypothesis Rick reasoned to*, not a finding. Everything
in this brief that is not explicitly cited is an assertion.

That is the actual purpose of this pilot. It is not a marketing campaign that happens to generate
research — **it is a research operation that happens to double as the sales motion.** Judge it on
whether it produces evidence, and treat the revenue outcome as a bonus that also happens to be
the kill criterion.

One consequence, folded into the plan below: the pilot must interview **the buyer as well as the
beneficiary**. Twenty business conversations tell us about the corridor. They tell us nothing
about how an agency actually produces its performance numbers today — which is the wedge. So a
parallel track of 3–5 agency-staff conversations runs from week 2. Without it, this lane gets
built on a guess about the buyer while all the evidence is about someone else.

# BRIEF

## 1. Corridor selection

### 1.1 The criteria

A corridor is the right test when it satisfies all seven. They are not weighted equally —
**#3 and #7 are disqualifying if absent**, because without a funded reporting obligation there is
no wedge to test, only a nice piece of journalism.

| # | Criterion | What "good" looks like | Why it matters to *this* test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Business density** | 60–120 ground-floor businesses within a walkable frame | Lets a 20-business census be a real census of a defined block-face, not a convenience sample |
| 2 | **Business mix** | 5+ distinct sectors; independent-majority; not one anchor's weather | A single-sector corridor produces one industry's story and no transferable method |
| 3 | **Identifiable corridor organization** | A named BID/BIA/association with a director and a meeting calendar | Someone to tell before starting (courtesy), and the credential that converts "unsanctioned" to "co-produced" later |
| 4 | **Evidence of recent grant activity or distress** | Named grant programs with named recipients in the last 5 years, or a dated distress event | No grants = no reporting obligation = no wedge. Distress gives the early-warning story something to have predicted |
| 5 | **Walkable in a day** | Continuous street-level storefronts, doors on the sidewalk, ≤15 blocks | Auto-oriented corridors destroy the correspondent cadence — you can't "drop in" on a strip mall |
| 6 | **Proximity to Rick** | ≤20 min door-to-door | The pilot needs ~9 field days plus quarterly returns *forever*. Drive time is the silent killer of continuity |
| 7 | **An agency already owes a report touching it** | A CDBG subrecipient, a philanthropic grant with funder reporting, or a BID with an assessment to justify | This is the wedge. If nobody is obliged to produce outcome data here, we are selling a vitamin |

*Source: criteria authored for this brief (Rick/Claude, 2026-09-23). Not drawn from an external framework. Assertion, not citation.*

### 1.3 The recommendation — first choice

> **El Cajon Boulevard, the City Heights segment.** Define the frame in week 1 as **every
> ground-floor business on both sides of El Cajon Blvd between roughly Fairmount Avenue and
> Euclid Avenue** — approximately the 4000 through 4700 blocks. Block numbers are approximate and
> **must be verified on the ground during the week-1 walk**; the walk sets the frame, and once
> set, the frame does not move.

**Why it wins:**

- **Density and mix.** The El Cajon Boulevard BIA covers roughly 60 blocks between Park Blvd and
  54th St and reports **over 1,000 small businesses** in membership. The City Heights stretch is
  the densest continuously-storefronted, independent-majority section of it — Mexican, Vietnamese,
  Somali, Ethiopian, and Iraqi-owned businesses side by side. That mix is the point: a finding
  that holds across five different immigrant business communities on the same block is a finding
  about *the corridor*, not about one trade.
  ([theboulevard.org](https://theboulevard.org/the-bid/about-bia/))
- **Corridor organization — three doors, not one.** The El Cajon Blvd Business Improvement
  Association (formed 1988), the City Heights Business Association, and City Heights CDC (offices
  at 4001 El Cajon Blvd — inside the frame). Note the City BID roster lists **two** El Cajon Blvd
  districts, *Central* (O-16919) and *Gateway* (O-16480); **verify in week 1 which ordinance
  contains the frame** before naming it in any document.
  ([City of San Diego BID roster](https://www.sandiego.gov/economic-development/about/bids))
- **Documented grant activity with a named cohort.** The **City Heights Business Relief Fund**
  raised nearly **$400,000** and made grants of **$4,000–$10,000 to 70 businesses**, with a
  selection committee spanning LISC San Diego, City Heights CDC, the International Rescue
  Committee, the City Heights Business Association, the El Cajon Blvd BIA, and U.S. Bank; funders
  included U.S. Bank Foundation, Price Philanthropies, SDG&E, Union Bank, The Hervey Family Fund
  at The San Diego Foundation, JP Morgan Chase, and Pacific Premier Bank.
  **That is the wedge, sitting there fully assembled**: eight funders, six intermediaries, seventy
  recipients, and an obligation on somebody to say what it bought.
  ([LISC San Diego](https://www.lisc.org/san-diego/impact/small-biz-relief/chbrf/))
- **Walkable, and walkable at the right hours.** A straight commercial boulevard with doors on
  the sidewalk. Twenty blocks is a morning. There is no version of this pilot that works in a
  corridor where you have to park twice.
- **Proximity.** Mid-city. Roughly 10–15 minutes from most of central San Diego. This matters more
  than it looks: the correspondent frame requires Rick to still be doing this in April.
- **A live reporting calendar.** The City of San Diego Economic Development Department administers
  both the BIDs and CDBG. The **FY2028 CDBG cycle is running right now** — a two-phase RFQ→RFP, with
  technical-assistance appointments bookable from **October 5, 2026**, and subrecipients owing
  year-end/closeout and monitoring performance reports (MPR indicators).
  A corridor report landing in **December 2026** lands while applicants are writing their case.
  ([City of San Diego CDBG applications](https://www.sandiego.gov/cdbg/apps))

**What is deliberately *not* claimed:** City Heights is **not** inside the federal San Diego
Promise Zone. The Promise Zone's 6.4 sq mi covers Barrio Logan, East Village, Sherman Heights,

Encanto. Do not imply the CDBG Promise Zone scoring bonus applies here.

### 1.4 The backup

### 1.5 Considered and set aside — San Ysidro

The strongest *distress* story in the county, and the wrong first test.

Border wait times at San Ysidro have run around **128 minutes average** with peaks far higher;
UCLA's Latino Policy & Politics Initiative counted **964 businesses** in the community in 2023,
most with fewer than 10 employees; border communities are estimated to lose roughly **$2 billion
annually** to excessive wait times, and Tijuana retail sales have been reported down as much as
**40%**.
([CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/california-divide/2024/04/mexico-border-california/) ·
[CBP border wait times](https://bwt.cbp.gov/details/09250401/POV) ·
[Gringo Gazette North](https://ggnorth.com/2026/07/border-wait-times-cut-tijuana-retail-sales-by-40/))

Three reasons to hold it: (a) **~20 miles each way** breaks criterion 6 and therefore breaks the
correspondent cadence; (b) it is **Spanish-dominant** — Rick's working Spanish is an unstated
assumption in this brief and needs an honest answer before, not after; (c) **the distress is
already extremely well documented**, so a 20-business study adds little marginal information,
which is precisely the Gate-2 kill condition in §7. San Ysidro is lane #2 once the method is
proven and there is a partner to run it with.

## 2. Why twenty

### 2.1 What the number has to carry

1. **A pattern a person can hold in their head.** "Nine of the twenty" is a sentence an executive
   director repeats in a meeting. "45% of surveyed businesses" is a sentence nobody repeats.
2. **One or two honest cuts** — by tenure (under 3 years vs over), and by whether they received
   assistance. Not more than that; see §2.3.
3. **Continuity that one person can sustain forever.** Twenty relationships × four touches a year
   = 80 conversations a year ≈ 1.5 a week. That is sustainable solo. Fifty is not.
4. **Enough that a refusal is data, not a hole.**

### 2.2 The challenge to the number — and the fix

Twenty is genuinely awkward. It is small for the wedge (grant reporting wants counts) and it is
large enough that someone will be tempted to report percentages off it. The fix is not a bigger n.
**The fix is to change what 20 is.**

> **Twenty is a census of a defined block-face, not a sample of a corridor.**

Define the frame as *every* ground-floor business on both sides of a named set of blocks — and if
that frame contains 26 businesses and 20 sit down, the report says: *26 in the frame, 20 spoke,
6 declined, here is the pattern in who declined.* That is a population Rick enumerated, not one he
estimated. It carries **no sampling error at all** — the uncertainty is non-response and
measurement, both of which can be shown honestly.

This is also why it is repeatable. A census of block-faces scales by adding block-faces. A sample
of a corridor has to be redesigned every time.

**Why not sample the whole 60-block BID?** Because a 20-business sample of 1,000+ businesses has a
95% confidence interval roughly **±22 percentage points** on a mid-range finding — a 45% result is
honestly "somewhere between 23% and 67%." That is not a number anyone can act on, and printing it
as "45%" would be the exact false-confidence failure the repo's own research guidance warns about.
*(Calculation: normal approximation, p=0.45, n=20. Real arithmetic, stated so it can be checked.)*

### 2.3 The small-numbers identification problem — decide the rules before the first interview

In a 20-business census on a **named block**, "the restaurant that lost its lease" identifies a
specific human being to anyone who walks past. These rules are not negotiable and go in the method
note:

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Two-tier permission** | Consent to *record* and consent to *quote by name* are separate questions with separate answers. Default is name-off. |
| **Cell suppression** | No aggregate cell smaller than **3** is printed. "2 of 20 said" becomes "a small number said" or is cut. |
| **No cross-tabs** | Industry × revenue direction on n=20 re-identifies nearly everyone. Single-variable counts only. |
| **Sensitive items aggregate-only** | Revenue direction, debt, landlord disputes, and anything that could damage the business appear only in aggregate, never attached to a name — even with permission. |
| **Immigration status: never** | Not asked, not recorded, not written down, not alluded to. If volunteered, the recorder goes off and it does not enter the file. |
| **Right to review and right to withdraw** | Every named quote is shown to its speaker before publication. Withdrawal is honored immediately and without argument, at any time, forever. |

**The trade Rick is making, stated plainly:** business-level attribution *on* (owners are proud of
their businesses and being named is often the reward), sensitive data attribution *off*. That
combination is what makes the business-facing version something people want a copy of, instead of
something they worry about.

## 3. What Rick actually does, week by week

### 3.1 The operating principle

A survey arrives once and takes. A correspondent is **continuously present, on first-name terms,
and files on a schedule whether or not there is news.** Three concrete behaviors make the
difference, and all three cost time rather than money:

1. **You come back.** The first visit is not an interview.
2. **You bring something every time.** A printed page, a photo you took, a name they asked for.
3. **You file on a cadence regardless.** A monthly one-page **Corridor Note**, handed or emailed to
   all twenty, even in a month when nothing happened. *Especially* in a month when nothing happened
   — that is what proves you are not there to extract something.

### 3.2 Getting in the door of a business that owes you nothing

**Two passes. Never ask for the interview on the first pass.**

- **Pass 1 (the walk).** Rick walks the frame with a clipboard and a phone camera, enumerates every
  ground-floor business, photographs each storefront, notes hours and apparent vacancy, and buys
  something small from four or five of them. He says who he is if asked and leaves. This produces
  the census frame *and* makes his face familiar before he asks for anything.
- **Pass 2 (the ask).** Return 3–6 days later, **at the right hour**, with a printed one-page
  description of the project and one printed photo of their own storefront to hand over.

**The hour rule — this decides the hit rate more than the script does:**

| Business type | Go | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Food service | 2:00–4:00pm | 11:30am–1:30pm; Friday after 4pm |
| Retail / salon / services | 10:00am–12:00pm | Saturdays; the 1st and last 2 days of the month |
| Auto / trades | 9:00–10:30am | End of day, any day |

*Source: general field-research practice plus Rick/Claude's reasoning about small-business rhythms. **Illustrative** — treat as a starting hypothesis and correct it from the week-1 walk, which is exactly what the walk is for.*

### 3.3 The doorway script — 60 seconds

Learn it cold, then stop performing it. The design rules: **name yourself, name the frame, name the
ask, name the return, name the exit — in that order, in under a minute, standing up.**

> *(Hand over the printed photo of their storefront first. Do not start talking until they have
> taken it.)*
>
> "Hi — I'm Rick. I live here in San Diego. I'm not selling anything and I'm not from the city.
>
> I'm writing up what's actually happening on this stretch of the Boulevard — these blocks here,
> between Fairmount and Euclid. I'm going to every business on both sides and asking the same
> handful of questions, and then I'm going to write it up and bring everyone a copy. You'd get it
> before anyone at the city does.
>
> It's about twenty minutes, and I'll stop at twenty whether we're done or not. Mostly it's
> practical stuff — how long you've been here, how this year's running compared to last, whether
> anybody's ever given you help and whether it actually helped.
>
> Now's probably bad. When's better — is there a slow hour?"

**Why each line is there:**
- *"I live here"* — not "I'm from a company." Proximity is the credential.
- *"I'm not selling anything and I'm not from the city"* — these are the two things they are
  already assuming. Say them before they have to wonder. **Both must remain true for the whole
  pilot.** The moment UpSight gets pitched in the corridor, the corridor closes.
- *"every business on both sides"* — signals census, not selection. Removes "why me?"
- *"I'll bring everyone a copy… before anyone at the city does"* — the return, and the sequencing
  promise that makes it trustworthy. **This is the single most important sentence in the script.**
- *"twenty minutes and I'll stop at twenty"* — a finite, checkable promise. Honoring it is what
  earns visit three.
- *"Now's probably bad. When's better?"* — hands them the graceful exit and converts a no into a
  scheduled yes. Roughly half of accepted interviews will come from this line, not from the ones
  above it. *(Assertion from general field practice — measure it and find out.)*

**When they say no:** "Totally fine — I'll leave this with you, and I'll drop the write-up by when
it's done either way." Then actually do it. A no in week 3 who receives the report in December is
a yes in the next wave.

### 3.4 Throughput and consent mechanics

| Item | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Interview length** | 20–25 min target, hard stop at 30. The hard stop is the promise; keeping it is the product. |
| **Per day realistically** | **3 completed** on a good day, counting walking, "come back Tuesday," interruptions from actual customers, and the two that evaporate. Plan 3; be pleased with 4. |
| **Field days needed** | 20 ÷ 3 ≈ 7, plus 2 for the tail = **9 field days.** |
| **Cadence** | 2 field days a week, same two weekdays every week. Predictability *is* the correspondent signal — they start expecting you. |
| **Recording** | Phone recorder placed visibly on the counter, screen up. If they flinch, it goes away and Rick takes notes — a notes-only interview is worth far more than a lost one. |
| **Consent** | One page, plain language, **English and Spanish minimum**, plus the corridor's other languages as identified in the walk. Plus a recorded 20-second verbal consent at the top of every tape: *"Just for the record — okay if I record this?"* and, separately, *"and can I use your business's name, or would you rather I didn't?"* |
| **Storage** | Recordings and transcripts into UpSight, one persistent person-profile per owner, evidence-linked. **This is the dogfood** — every friction hit goes to `10-ops/dogfooding-log.md`. |
| **Language** | Assume Rick cannot cover the corridor alone. Budget a bilingual walking partner for 3 of the 9 field days (§6). A mistranslated interview is worse than a missing one. |

## 4. What gets asked — the interview spine

**Standing rules,** enforced from `40-gtm/channels/content-seo/glossary/leading-question.md` and
`discovery-interview.md`:

- **Past-tense and episode-specific.** Never "would you," never "do you think," never "how
  frustrating is." Always "walk me through the last time."
- **No adjectives in the question.** "How's business?" not "how tough has it been?"
- **Never name the answer you want.** Do not say "barriers," "distress," "technical assistance,"
  "ecosystem," or "stakeholder." Say *help*, *money*, *paperwork*, *slow*, *stopped*.
- **Rick talks under a third of the time.** Count it on the tape afterwards; it is the single best
  quality check available.
- **Silence after the hard questions.** The first answer to Q13 is the polite one.

The spine is six sections. **Sections 2 and 3 are the grant-reporting wedge. Section 4 is the
early-warning retention story.** If time runs out, cut Section 5 first, then Q6.

### Section 1 — Who and how long (3 min, builds the census)

**Q1.** "How long have you been here, in this space?"
*→ Tenure. The single best predictor variable in a 20-business census, and the only cut that is safe to print.*

**Q2.** "How did you end up on this block, specifically?"
*→ Opens the person up, and reliably produces the corridor's own history — who was here before, what the block used to be. Also the first place a landlord story surfaces.*

**Q3.** "How many people work here right now, counting you? And about a year ago?"
*→ **The jobs number.** Ask for headcount, never for "FTEs" — Rick does the 40-hours-equals-one conversion himself afterwards, which is how CDBG counts it. Follow-up: "full-time, part-time, or a mix?" and "family, or hired?"*
([HUD's FTE convention](https://www.hudexchange.info/resource/2376/cdbg-economic-development-toolkit/))

### Section 2 — The last twelve months, as events (6 min — wedge)

**Q4.** "Is this year running ahead of last year, behind it, or about the same?"
*→ Revenue direction. Three-way, neutral, no adjectives, no dollar figures requested — asking for revenue numbers ends the interview and produces lies besides. Direction is what the reporting actually needs.*

**Q5.** "What moved it?"
*→ The causal story in their words. Do not supply candidates. Whatever they name first is the finding.*

**Q6.** *(first to cut)* "What's the busiest you've been this year, and what was going on that week?"
*→ Anchors the direction answer to something concrete and checkable.*

### Section 3 — Help received, and whether it worked (7 min — the wedge proper)

**Q7.** "In the last couple of years, has anyone — the city, a nonprofit, a bank, the business
association — given you money, a loan, or help with something? Walk me through what happened."
*→ **Assistance received.** The list of sources is deliberately broad and deliberately unranked so nothing is cued. Follow-up: "anyone else?" — most owners forget one.*

**Q8.** "What did you have to do to get it?"
*→ **The cost of compliance**, which no agency measures and every owner remembers in detail. This is where the most quotable material in the whole study comes from.*

**Q9.** "Did you get out of it what you expected?"
*→ **Efficacy.** Neutral phrasing; permits "yes," "no," and the common and most useful answer, "sort of, but not the part I needed."*

**Q10.** "Would you do it again?"
*→ The behavioral version of a satisfaction score, and far harder to answer politely.*

**Q11.** "Has there been anything you started applying for and didn't finish?"
*→ **The abandonment question.** Assert: this is the sharpest finding in most corridor studies and almost nobody has it, because **agencies can only see completed applications.** An agency that learns nine of twenty started its program and quit at the same step has just been handed something it cannot get any other way. If one question justifies the whole pilot, it is this one.*

### Section 4 — Leading indicators (5 min — the retention story)

Every one of these is a fact with a date, not an opinion. Together they are the early-warning
instrument. **None of them are presented to the agency in the first meeting** (see §8).

**Q12.** "When does your lease run out?"
*→ The best single predictor of a corridor turning. Concrete, non-sensitive, easy to answer. Follow-up: "have you talked to the landlord about after that?"*

**Q13.** "What did you stop doing this year that you used to do?"
*→ Quiet contraction. Catches shortened hours, dropped product lines, a delivery service cancelled, a second location given up. Owners almost never volunteer these as problems. **Sit in the silence.***

**Q14.** "When did you last hire someone, and is that person still here?"
*→ Hiring and churn as a dated event rather than a plan.*

**Q15.** "Has anything changed in what your suppliers ask of you?"
*→ Credit terms tightening is an early and underused distress signal, and owners will discuss it freely because it is someone else's decision.*

**Q16.** "What's the last thing you needed to get done for the business that took way longer than it should have?"
*→ **Barriers, without using the word "barrier."** Produces permits, inspections, bank paperwork, landlords, the DMV, a broken sign — the actual texture. Compare with what the agency's programs are designed to fix; the gap is a finding.*

### Section 5 — The block (3 min — the corridor sensor)

**Q17.** "Which places on this block have come and gone in the last couple of years?"
*→ Turns each respondent into a sensor on the whole frame, and validates the census. Twenty independent accounts of the same block-face is a genuinely strong dataset — and it is how vacancies get dated without permits.*

**Q18.** "Who else on this block should I talk to? Would you walk me over?"
*→ Referral chaining. The walk-over is worth ten cold doors.*

### Section 6 — Close (2 min)

**Q19.** "What should I have asked you and didn't?"

Then, in order and without rushing:
1. Thank them.
2. **Permissions, separately:** *"Two things — can I use your business's name when I write this up? And is there anything you told me you'd rather I kept out?"*
3. **Deliver the return, with a date:** *"I'll bring you a copy in December. You'll see it before the city does."*
4. **Set the next touch:** *"I'll drop by with a one-pager in a few weeks either way."*

**There is no step 5.** UpSight is not mentioned in the corridor. Not once, not softly, not at
the end. The tool is how the work gets done, not what the conversation is for — and the moment a
business owner concludes Rick was collecting them for a software company, the corridor closes and
the lane closes with it.

### The parallel agency track (3–5 conversations, weeks 2–8)

Same discipline, different room. Targets: a City EDD Community Development staffer (start with
the CDBG technical-assistance appointment), a LISC San Diego program officer, a City Heights CDC
program manager, a BID executive director, and one grant-writer who serves several of them.

The only four questions that matter:

- "Walk me through the last performance report you filed. What was in it?"
- "Where did those numbers come from?" *(then stop talking)*
- "Which number in it were you least confident about?"
- "What happened the last time a funder asked you something you couldn't answer from what you had?"

*This track is the buyer evidence. **Without it the lane is a guess.** It is also the cheapest part
of the entire pilot — five conversations, a day and a half total.*

### A — The Corridor Report

Cover states, before anything else: the named blocks, the dates walked, businesses in the frame,
conversations completed, refusals, and who paid for it (**nobody — self-funded**). That last line
does more work than the next ten pages.

Contents, in order:
1. **The census.** Who is actually on this block-face, by type and tenure — *counted by walking it*.
   **Lead with the discrepancy against the city's own business-license file.** Assert: there will
   be one, it will be material, and demonstrating that the official list is wrong in the first two
   pages establishes in about thirty seconds why walking beats querying. If there turns out to be
   no discrepancy, say so — that is also a finding, and an honest one.
2. **Employment.** Headcount now vs twelve months prior, converted to FTE on HUD's convention,
   self-reported, method stated on the figure.
3. **Direction of trade.** Ahead / behind / same, with the reasons owners gave, in their words.
4. **Assistance received — and what it cost to get.** The program-by-program picture including
   Q8's compliance burden.
5. **The abandonment finding.** Who started an application and stopped, and at which step.
6. **Barriers, ranked by unprompted mentions.** Unprompted is the whole point; say so on the chart.
7. **The watchlist.** Leading indicators, presented as *dated observations* rather than predictions.
8. **What I could not find out.** A real section, honestly written.
9. **The refusal log** (G) and **the method note** (H).

### B and C — the versions for the businesses, which are the actual point

**Nobody does this.** A consultant delivers upward and the corridor never sees the study it
produced. Rick delivers downward **first**, by hand, in December, in the languages of the corridor.

- **B, the snapshot:** two pages. *"Here's what your nineteen neighbours said."* Big type, plain
  words, three or four charts, no jargon. Something an owner will actually tape up by the register.
- **C, the one-pagers:** each business gets its own page — their profile as recorded, their quotes
  as they will appear, and an explicit invitation to **correct the record**. Corrections are
  welcomed, not tolerated; a returned correction is the strongest possible signal the relationship
  is real.

**This is what earns conversation two**, and conversation two is what makes the next wave cheap.
It is also the honest reciprocation for twenty minutes of a working person's day.

### E — The Reporting Crosswalk (the artifact that closes the deal)

One table. Left column: the data element Rick collected. Right column: **the field it fills in the
agency's own performance reporting** — businesses assisted, FTE jobs created/retained, LMI
beneficiaries, barriers to access, outcomes of assistance. Reporting obligations are real and
dated: HUD entitlement jurisdictions file a CAPER **within 90 days of the end of the program year**,
and CDBG subrecipients owe monitoring performance reports and closeout reports.
([HUD CAPER requirement](https://www.hudexchange.info/programs/cdbg-cv/cdbg-cv-toolkit/eligible-uses/economic-development/) ·
[City of San Diego subrecipient reporting](https://www.sandiego.gov/cdbg/apps))

The agency reads its own obligation in Rick's column headers. Nothing needs to be argued after that.

### What makes it obviously better than a consultant's study

| | Consultant's corridor study | This |
|---|---|---|
| **Frame** | Convenience sample, frame undisclosed | Census of a **named block-face**, every business enumerated |
| **Traceability** | Findings summarized; quotes decorative | **Every claim links to the verbatim that produced it** |
| **Shelf life** | Dead on delivery; a PDF with a year on it | Dated, live, **updated quarterly because he's still there** |
| **Non-response** | Hidden | **Published**, with the pattern in who declined |
| **Inconvenient findings** | Softened; the client is the funder | Abandoned applications and assistance that didn't help, printed |
| **Who sees it first** | The commissioning agency | **The businesses**, in person, in their languages |
| **Method** | Proprietary | **Published**, reproducible |
| **Cost to the agency** | $40–120K and four months | **Zero, and it already exists** |

*Source: right column is this pilot's design commitment. Left column is Rick/Claude's characterization of typical commissioned corridor studies — **illustrative and asserted, not measured**. If a specific competing study is cited in sales material, read it first and quote it accurately.*

**The compounding claim, stated carefully.** After one pass this is a good study. Its actual
advantage appears at pass two, when the same twenty people are asked the same questions against
their own prior answers and the report can say *what changed, per business, with both quotes side
by side.* A consultant's second study starts from zero. **That is the compounding-intelligence
differentiator, and the pilot cannot prove it — one pass cannot demonstrate compounding.** What the
pilot can do is make pass two cheap and obvious. Do not oversell this in December.

## 7. Success criteria, decided in advance

**Written on 2026-09-23, before a single door is knocked.** The date matters — these are not
adjustable once results start arriving. Each gate is recorded in `00-control/status.md` on the day
it is assessed, pass or fail.

### Gate 1 — Field feasibility · end of week 4 (≈ Oct 23, 2026)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Continue if** | ≥50% of doors approached (pass 2) produce a sit-down of ≥15 min, **and** ≥3 of the first 8 interviews volunteer an unprompted specific about assistance received or an abandoned application |
| **Stop / re-site if** | <30% door conversion after 25 approaches |

<30% is not shyness — it is the wrong corridor or the wrong script. Diagnose which in one day,
then move to the Diamond backup **once and only once**. Failing Gate 1 costs ~6 days total. That
cheapness is the point of putting it at week 4.

### Gate 2 — Signal · end of week 8 (≈ Nov 20, 2026), at n=20

| | |
|---|---|
| **Continue if** | At least one finding is both (a) stated independently by **≥6 of 20** and (b) **not present in any published City, BID, CDBG, LISC, or CDC document Rick can find** |
| **Kill if** | Everything the twenty said is already in the public record |

Condition (b) is the harder half and it is the real test. **If the corridor's information problem
is already solved, UpSight is not needed here** — and that is a genuine, respectable outcome worth
knowing for $2,000. Rick must actually do the literature search before claiming novelty; the
search itself goes in the method note.

### Gate 3 — Commercial · 6 weeks after delivery (≈ Feb 27, 2027)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Continue if** | ≥2 of 5 target agencies take a **second** meeting after receiving the report, **and** ≥1 names a budget line, a fiscal year, and a person who signs |

### THE KILL CRITERION

> **If, six weeks after the finished report is in the hands of five named agencies, zero of them
> have named a budget line and a signer, Rick stops the lane.**
>
> Not "iterates the pitch." Not "tries a different corridor." **Stops.** He publishes the corridor
> report anyway — it is good, it cost $2,135, and it is a real content asset — delivers every
> business its copy, writes the finding into `20-research/`, closes the question in
> `00-control/decisions.md`, and returns to the AI-tools lane.
>
> **The reasoning:** a finished, free, obviously useful, evidence-linked corridor study that
> cannot produce a single budget conversation in six weeks has not failed on procurement, on
> packaging, or on polish. **It has failed on demand.** No better artifact fixes that, and a solo
> founder cannot afford to find out slowly.

### Signals this lane is a mirage — watch for these throughout, not only at the gates

1. **Universal enthusiasm, zero budget lines.** The classic econ-dev trap. Program staff love it,
   nobody can name who signs. Enthusiasm from people without spending authority is not traction.
2. **"Could you do another one for us — as a partnership?"** Translation: free. Say yes once only
   if it comes with a named signer attached to the next one.
3. **The praise is all about the report's design and none about its findings.** Means the content
   told them nothing they did not know. That is Gate 2 failing late.
4. **Everything routes to "apply to our RFP next year."** A 12–18 month cycle. Real, but it cannot
   be the *only* path — a solo founder cannot finance it.
5. **They want the data but not the relationship.** They would buy a study, not a subscription.
   This one is the most dangerous because it *looks* like a win: it kills the compounding-
   intelligence differentiator and converts UpSight into a one-man consulting shop with a
   software licence attached.
6. **The BID goes quiet after the courtesy call.** Silence from the corridor organization usually
   means territorial friction being handled somewhere Rick cannot see. Address it directly and
   early (§9.2).

### 8.2 What the meeting looks like when you arrive with a finished study

You are not pitching. **You are delivering something they already own a copy of.** That inversion
is the entire advantage; do not spend it by presenting.

1. **Hand it over. Say almost nothing.** "This is the corridor. Twenty businesses, every one on
   both sides of those blocks. The businesses got theirs two weeks ago."
2. **Let them read.** They will go to the census page and the employment numbers first. Do not
   narrate. Do not apologize for anything.
3. **Ask exactly one question, then stop talking:**

   > **"When you file your next performance report, where do these numbers come from today?"**

   Then sit in the silence. *Assert: the honest answer is usually some version of "we ask the
   subrecipients and they estimate," or "we don't really have that." That answer is the wedge
   opening itself, in their own words, and it is worth ten minutes of positioning.*
4. **Turn to the crosswalk (artifact E).** Their reporting fields in Rick's column headers. Nothing
   needs arguing after this page.
5. **Then the offer, framed as relief rather than a product:**

   > "I did twenty of these for free, in twelve weeks, alone. What I'd want to do next is a hundred,
   > four times a year, across two corridors — and hand you the fields already filled before your
   > filing date."

6. **Ask for the specifics in the same meeting, not in a follow-up email:** *"Which budget would
   this come out of, which fiscal year, and who signs it?"* If those three answers do not exist,
   note it — it is Gate-3 evidence.

### 8.3 What to ask for

> across two corridors**. That is **below cost** — that scope is roughly 30–35 working days, about
> $24K–$28K of founder time. Pricing is now owned by

**Not a contract. Not an RFP response. A paid pilot, priced from the offer table in
`30-strategy/econ-dev-pricing-2026-09.md`, with a named deliverable tied to their actual filing

- **Offer B — the Filing Package, $14,500.** One full filing period, 25 conversations, two filings,

Match the scope to the price. Do not quote a number and then agree to a scope that does not fit it —

### 8.4 Where early warning goes

**Not in the first meeting. Not on the cover. Not in the crosswalk.**

Leading with early warning makes Rick sound speculative — he is predicting things about a corridor
he has walked for one quarter, to people who have worked it for fifteen years. Leading with grant
reporting makes him sound like **relief from a chore they are already obliged to perform and
currently do badly.**

Early warning comes out on the **second or third meeting, or at renewal**, in roughly this form:

> "The reason to keep this running rather than repeat it once a year is the lease dates and the
> supplier terms. Half a dozen of these leases come up in the same two quarters. You'd have known
> the Fairmount block was turning well before the sales-tax data showed it."

That sentence is worth a renewal. **In the first meeting it is worth nothing**, because there is no
track record behind it yet — which is exactly why it is the retention story and not the wedge.

## 9. Risks

### 9.1 Businesses refuse to talk

**Likely enough to plan around, not fatal.** Mitigations: the two-pass approach (§3.2) so the ask
is never cold; the hour rule (§3.2), which probably matters more than the script; referral chaining
with a walk-over (Q18); handing over their storefront photo before speaking; and **the frame
design itself** — in a census, a refusal is a recorded observation rather than a missing row.
Publish the refusal log (artifact G). If it looks like Gate 1 will fail, diagnose *language* before
diagnosing *script* — on a polyglot corridor that is the far more likely cause, and it is fixable
with a partner and $200.

### 9.2 The agency feels territorial about unsanctioned work in "their" corridor

**The biggest non-obvious risk, and the one most likely to be handled badly.** An unsolicited study
of somebody's corridor can read as a challenge to their competence. Six mitigations, all cheap:

1. **Tell the BIA in week 1, before the first interview — not after.** A ten-minute call: *"I'm
   doing this either way and I'd rather you heard it from me. You'll get a copy the same week the
   businesses do, and I'll take corrections."* Being told beforehand converts a potential enemy
   into a party with advance notice.
2. **Never say "study," "assessment," or "evaluation" in the corridor.** Say *notes* and *write-up*.
   Those words are not a euphemism — they are an accurate description of what a correspondent files,
   and they carry no audit connotation.
3. **The businesses get it first,** so by the time any agency reads it, its own constituents are
   already positive about it. This is the strongest available defense and it costs nothing.
4. **Name their programs accurately and generously.** If the Storefront Improvement Program or the
   Relief Fund helped someone, that goes near the front with the owner's own words.
5. **Never print a criticism of a named program without that program's response printed beside it.**
   Offer the right of reply in writing, with a deadline, and print "declined to comment" if it comes
   to that — but offer it first, every time.
6. **Credit the corridor organization by name for what it actually does.** The BIA has been at this
   since 1988; the report should read as though Rick knows that.

### 9.3 Consent and privacy exposure

**Real, and sharpest here because of the small-numbers problem (§2.3).** The rule set in §2.3 is
the mitigation and it is binding: two-tier permission, cell suppression at n<3, no cross-tabs,
sensitive items aggregate-only, immigration status never, review before publication, withdrawal
honored without argument at any time. Two additions: **recordings and transcripts stay in UpSight
and are not shared with any agency, ever** — agencies receive findings and approved quotes, never
raw material; and the one-page consent form says, in plain language and in the corridor's
languages, exactly who will see what. If any of this feels like it is slowing the work down, that
is the feeling of doing it correctly.

### 9.4 Rick's time consumed for a quarter

**The largest real cost (§6.4).** Mitigations: the hard 2-days-a-week cap; Gate 1 at week 4 so a
bad corridor costs six days rather than twelve weeks; the AI-tools lane's live experiments stay
untouched and keep their owner; and one explicit constraint —

> **This brief does not authorize proceeding past Gate 1 without Gate 1 actually being assessed and
> written into `00-control/status.md`.** Skipping the gate because the fieldwork is going well is
> the specific failure mode this document exists to prevent.

### 9.5 The lane has no customer evidence behind it

Restated from the top because it is a risk and not merely a caveat. The entire premise — that
grant reporting is the funded, mandatory pain and that early warning is what drives renewal — is
**reasoning, not evidence**. The parallel agency track (§4) is the mitigation and it is the highest-
value 1.5 days in the whole plan. If those five conversations reveal that agencies do *not*
experience reporting as painful, or that the pain sits with a subrecipient nobody will pay for,
**that finding arrives in week 8 and it changes everything** — which is the correct time for it to
arrive. Run the agency track early. Do not let it slip behind the fieldwork just because the
fieldwork is more fun.

### 9.6 A smaller one worth naming — the solo-dependency risk

Every relationship in this corridor is with Rick personally. That is the correspondent frame
working as designed, and it is also a single point of failure that makes the lane hard to hand off
or sell. Not solvable in the pilot. **Name it now** so it is not discovered as a surprise in
month nine.
