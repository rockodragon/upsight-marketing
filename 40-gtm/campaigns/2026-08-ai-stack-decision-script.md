# Survey + Interview Script — "How did you actually pick?"

**Objective:** Learn what small-company operators actually check before committing to an AI tool — the trigger, who had to be convinced, the criteria they used, the ones they skipped, and what they'd check first next time.
**Target:** Owners, COOs/GMs, and fractional operators at companies under ~200 people who have committed to at least one AI tool in the last 18 months. Non-technical or semi-technical. **Not** engineers choosing a model.
**Time:** Tier 1 — 2 minutes. Tier 2 — 15 minutes, hard-stopped.
**Method:** Tier 1 public ask via UpSight, posted to LinkedIn. Tier 2 conversational UpSight interview — respondent picks voice, video, or chat.

> **Standing rules.** No jargon — no "model," "stack," "eval," "inference," "vendor
> lock-in." Say *tool*, *check*, *get out of it*. Never ask which tool is best; the
> names come out on their own. Never state Rick's own leaning.

---

## The interview request (doubles as outbound first-touch)

This is the highest-value piece of copy in the campaign — it's a cold email with a
much better ask than a demo request.

**Subject:** how you picked your AI tools — 15 min, for a piece I'm writing

> Hi {{first_name}},
>
> [PERSONALIZE — one real line about their practice or a recent post.]
>
> I'm writing something on how operators at small companies actually decide which
> AI tools to commit to — not which tool wins, but what people genuinely check
> before they sign. Every write-up I can find is either a vendor pitch or a
> ranking, and neither is how anyone I know actually makes the call.
>
> I'm interviewing 10–12 people. 15 minutes, and I'll hard-stop at 15. You'd see
> anything I quote before it goes out, and I'll send you the full synthesis — what
> the other 11 said — before it's public.
>
> Worth 15 minutes in the next two weeks?
>
> Rick

*Why it works: the ask is their expertise, not their budget. It's specific and finite. The return — seeing what 11 peers said — is real and costs us nothing. Log the reply in UpSight either way; a "no thanks, but here's my quick take" is still evidence.*

## TIER 1 — The public ask

### Post copy

> Everyone is picking their AI tools this year. Almost nobody can tell you how they
> decided.
>
> Genuine question for anyone who runs a company or advises one:
>
> **What's the last AI tool you actually committed to — and what did you check
> before you committed?**
>
> Not which tool. What you checked. (And if the honest answer is "someone I trust
> was using it" — say that. I suspect it's the most common one and nobody writes
> it down.)
>
> I'm collecting answers properly and publishing the criteria set, plus what people
> said they'd check first next time. 2 minutes → [link]

*Production note: line 1 is the whole hook — it names something everyone is quietly doing and nobody has language for. Giving explicit permission to answer "I copied someone I trust" is what makes the herd data honest; without it, everyone reports a tidy process they didn't follow.*

### Q1 (the one question)
> Think about the last AI tool your company actually committed to — paid for, rolled
> out, or built a process around. What did you check before you committed?

*Type: open-ended (voice / video / text)*
*Insight target: the criteria, in their own words, anchored to a real past commitment. "Committed to" filters out tools they merely tried.*

### Q2 (AI interviewer follow-up — fires if Q1 is thin)
> — Was there anything you meant to check and didn't get to?
> — Who else had to be OK with it?
> — Anything you'd check first if you were doing it again?

*Insight target: turns a one-liner into usable evidence. Also the on-camera proof that a form can't do this — capture it for asset F.*

### Q3 (tagging — required)
> Which is closest to you?
> Owner / founder · COO, GM, or ops lead · Fractional or independent consultant ·
> Department head at a larger company · Something else

*Type: multiple choice*
*Insight target: the right-room check. Target ≥60% in the first three buckets. If this comes back full of engineers and department heads at big companies, the post copy pulled wrong — fix the copy, not the topic.*

### Q4 (closer)
> Anything about that decision you wish someone had told you beforehand?

*Type: open-ended, optional*
*Insight target: the regret question. In practice this is where the best pull-quote comes from.*

**Then:** *"Mind if I quote you by name when I publish? (Yes / First name only / Anonymous)"*

## TIER 2 — Operator interview (15 min, hard stop)

### Introduction

> Thanks for the time. Quick context: I run a small company and I keep getting asked
> by clients which AI tools to commit to — and I noticed I couldn't give a straight
> answer about how *I'd* decide. So I'm asking a dozen people who've actually made
> the call.
>
> There's no right answer and I'm not testing you. I'm after how it really went,
> including the messy parts — that's the useful bit and it's the part nobody
> publishes. I'll stop at fifteen minutes, and you'll see anything I quote before
> it goes out.

*Says nothing about UpSight and nothing about Rick's own leaning. Both would contaminate the answers. Framing is peer-to-peer — he has the same problem, not a clipboard.*

---

### Section 1 — The last real decision

#### Q1
> Take me back to the last AI tool your company actually committed to. What was
> going on that made you go looking in the first place?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: the real trigger. Past behavior, specific event. Distinguishes "a problem got bad enough" from "everyone was talking about it" without asking which.*
*Follow-ups: "Roughly when?" · "Was that you, or was someone pushing for it?" · "What were you doing before?"*

#### Q2
> Who else had to be OK with it before you could go ahead?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: the decision unit — who signs, who vetoes, who has to actually use it. Critical for a business audience and completely absent from any technical framing. Directly useful to outbound: it tells us who else to multi-thread.*
*Follow-ups: "Who was hardest to convince?" · "What did they need to hear?"*

#### Q3
> What did you actually check before you committed?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: **the central question of the whole campaign.** Plain language on purpose — "check" invites a list of real actions rather than a tidy framework.*
*Follow-ups: "What did you look at first?" · "What did you take someone's word for instead of checking yourself?" · "How long did the whole thing take, start to finish?"*

---

### Section 2 — The criteria people don't say out loud

#### Q4
> What made you nervous about it?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: surfaces lock-in, vendor survival, data worries, and team-adoption risk **without naming any of them**. Naming them would hand the respondent our hypothesis; this way whatever comes up came up on its own, which is the only version worth publishing.*
*Follow-ups: "Did that turn out to be the right thing to worry about?"*

#### Q5
> Was there an option you seriously looked at and turned down? What ruled it out?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: rejection criteria — usually sharper and more honest than selection criteria. People rationalize what they chose; they remember exactly why they killed something.*

#### Q6 — the herd question
> How much did it matter that people you respect were already using it?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: **social proof as a decision criterion.** Asking "did you just follow the crowd?" gets a defensive no every time. Asking "how much did it matter" presumes it mattered somewhat and makes the honest answer the comfortable one — the social-desirability antidote. Expect this to be one of the top criteria and largely unadmitted elsewhere. That gap is the piece's sharpest finding.*
*Follow-ups: "Who specifically?" · "Where did you come across that?" · "Would you have looked at it otherwise?"*

---

### Section 3 — What it actually cost

#### Q7 *(first to cut if running long)*
> A year in — what's it cost you that you didn't expect?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: the gap between the price at signing and the real cost — seat creep, admin time, retraining, the thing nobody budgeted. Past-tense and quantifiable.*
*Follow-ups: "Ballpark, versus what you thought going in?" · "Anyone's time you didn't count?"*

#### Q8
> If you had to get out of it tomorrow, what would that take?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: **lock-in, made concrete.** Asked as a thought exercise about a real system rather than a preference question, so the answer is an inventory — data, retraining, rebuilt processes, contract term — not an opinion. Almost nobody checks this before signing, which is precisely what makes it publishable.*
*Follow-ups: "Would you get your data out?" · "Did you think about that beforehand?"*

#### Q9 — the deliverable
> Knowing what you know now, what would you check first next time?

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: **the criteria they earned.** This is the literal headline of the published piece and the single most quotable question in the set. Sit in the silence after asking it — the first answer is usually the polite one.*

---

### Section 4 — Sources

#### Q10
> Whose judgment do you actually trust on this stuff? Not the loudest — who you'd
> call.

*Type: open-ended*
*Insight target: two jobs. Maps where this audience really gets information (directly usable for distribution), and produces the snowball recruiting list — every name is a warm intro request.*
*Follow-up: "Would you introduce me?"*

---

### Closing

> That's everything. Anything I should have asked and didn't?

Then, in order:
1. Thank them.
2. **Permission:** *"Two asks — can I quote you by name, and can I use a clip of the
   bit about [specific thing they said]? You'll see both before anything goes out."*
3. **Deliver the return:** *"I'll send you the full synthesis before it's public —
   you'll see what the other 11 said first."* Honor this. It's why they said yes.
4. **The soft second meeting — only if they've leaned in:** *"This whole thing ran
   through the tool I build — it's what turned these conversations into the themes.
   Want me to show you what it did with yours?"* Offer once. Don't push.

## Interviewer notes

**What to listen for**
- **The gap between the stated process and the real one.** People describe an
  orderly evaluation first and admit at minute eleven that a peer's recommendation
  did most of the work. Q6 and Q9 are the crowbars.
- **"We just needed something."** Urgency-driven decisions with no criteria at all
  are a real and underreported category. Don't let the respondent tidy it up — that
  answer is more useful than a fake framework.
- **Anything they checked that surprised you.** The unexpected criteria are the
  reason the piece is worth publishing rather than another checklist.
- **The second-order pain.** When someone says "we still do X by hand" or "nobody
  actually uses it," that's both a finding and a qualification signal. Note it.

**Traps specific to this audience**
- **Post-hoc tidying.** Everyone rationalizes a decision as more rigorous than it
  was. Antidote: always ask what they *did*, never what they *considered*.
- **Not wanting to look unsophisticated.** A non-technical operator may perform
  diligence they didn't do. Antidote: normalize the shortcut in the question itself
  — Q6 does this deliberately, and the public post copy does it too.
- **Jargon drift — ours.** The moment we say "vendor lock-in" or "evaluation
  criteria," they start answering in consultant-speak instead of their own words,
  and the quotes go dead. Stay plain the whole way through.
- **Turning it into a demo.** The interview is not a pitch. UpSight comes up once,
  at the end, only if they've leaned in. Breaking this makes the next ten interview
  requests harder to get.

**Time discipline**
Sections 1 and 2 are the campaign. If you're at minute ten, drop Q7, go straight to
Q8, Q9, Q10 and close. **Ending at fifteen minutes as promised is what earns the
second meeting** — and the second meeting is what this campaign is actually for.
