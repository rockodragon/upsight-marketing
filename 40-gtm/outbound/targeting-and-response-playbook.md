## What UpSight Does — the "so what"

UpSight helps organizations build **customer intelligence** — understand what their customers want, need, and struggle with. This lets them do two things: **(1)** make better decisions about products, services, and pricing, and **(2)** create honest, real content for marketing and investors (testimonials, impact reports). UpSight turns research — both qualitative (interviews, open answers) and quantitative (numbers, ratings) — into key themes. Then it turns those themes into ranked actions, so you can run the business based on what customers actually say.

## TL;DR Operating Cadence

(TL;DR means "too long; didn't read" — this is the quick summary. Cadence means the timing and spacing of your outreach.)

- **Every day:** send 20 emails total (new first-touches plus scheduled follow-ups, from one mailbox). Weight sends toward Tuesday–Thursday, mid-morning in the recipient's local time.
- **Per contact:** 4–6 touches over 10–21 days. Mix email with 1–2 LinkedIn touches.
- **Every email:** must have one real, specific, sourced hook (a fact about them you can point to) — or it does not go out. This is the same rule `templates.md` already enforces.
- **Every week:** check reply rate, positive-reply rate, and meetings booked — broken down **by segment**, not just as one overall number. Stop segments that don't work; do more of what works.

### Apollo firmographic filters + intent signals, by segment

**A. Consultants / fractional operators**
- **Company filters:** Industry = *Management Consulting* or *Business Consulting and Services*; company size 1–25 employees (most are solo or small). There isn't much company data (firmographics — facts about a company like size, industry, or revenue) for this group, so instead search by **person-level job title**: `fractional COO`, `fractional CMO`, `fractional Head of Research`, `independent consultant`, `principal consultant`, `insights consultant`, `research consultant`.
- **Intent signals** (signs that someone is likely ready to buy soon): company description contains words like "discovery," "voice of customer," or "research sprints"; or a **job-change filter** — someone moved into a "Founder/Principal" title at a 1-person company in the last 12 months (they just went independent and are rebuilding how they deliver work for clients).
- **San Diego angle:** filter for the San Diego metro area first — this is the easiest segment to close in person (see §5).

**B. Founders / product teams (PMF)**
- **Company filters:** Industry = Software, Internet, or SaaS; company size 11–200 employees; **funding filter** = raised Seed through Series B funding in the last 12–18 months (fresh money means pressure to prove product-market fit).
- **Intent signals:** **job postings** from the last 90 days for "User Researcher," "Product Researcher," "Head of Product," or "Product Ops"; a **job-change filter** — someone was just hired into one of those roles (this means someone now owns the research problem); a **technographic filter** (a filter based on which tools a company uses) on Notion, Dovetail, or Airtable — a sign they already do informal research and want to do it properly.

**C. Sales orgs (deal intelligence)**
- **Company filters:** Industry = B2B SaaS or Technology; company size 50–500 employees; revenue $5M–$100M.
- **Intent signals:** job-change filter — a VP Sales, Head of Revenue, or Director of Sales Ops hired in the last 90 days (new leaders often bring in new tools); job postings for "Sales Enablement" or "Revenue Operations"; a technographic filter for Gong, Chorus, or Clari. Decide on purpose whether to skip companies that already use these tools (harder to replace) or target them anyway and describe UpSight as a companion tool — CRM-linked evidence, not call-recording analytics.

**D. Customer Success (onboarding / CSAT / churn)**
- **Company filters:** Industry = SaaS or Technology, with a PLG (product-led growth — customers try the product themselves before buying) or high-touch onboarding style; company size 50–500 employees; revenue $5M–$100M.
- **Intent signals:** job-change filter — a VP/Director of Customer Success or Director of CX (customer experience) hired in the last 90 days; a technographic filter for Gainsight, Vitally, Catalyst, or Planhat (a sign they already value organized customer-success data — good ground for an evidence layer); job postings that mention "churn," "onboarding," or "customer health."

**E. Nonprofits (donor development)**
- **Company filters:** Industry = *Nonprofit Organization Management*, *Fund-Raising*, or *Philanthropy*; company size 5–200 employees.
- **Intent signals:** job-change filter — a Development Director or Director of Advancement hired in the last 90–180 days (nonprofit hiring moves slower, so use a wider window); a technographic filter for Bloomerang, Neon CRM, Raiser's Edge, DonorPerfect, or Salesforce NPSP (a sign of an organized, data-driven fundraising team).
- **San Diego angle:** filter for San Diego County first. It's a smaller list, but your local network and overlap with EvoNexus/CONNECT give you warm introductions that a cold list never could.

**F. Event & community organizers**
- **Company filters:** Industry = *Events Services*, *Hospitality*, *Leisure Travel & Tourism*, *Marketing and Advertising* (agencies that run in-person experiences), or *Non-Profit Organization Management* (membership groups and conference producers); company size 2–100 employees.
- **Intent signals:** job postings for "Event Producer/Coordinator," "Community Manager," "Sponsorship Manager," or "Partnerships Director"; also check by hand against the San Diego Convention Center's roughly 90 major yearly events and the San Diego Brewers Guild festival calendar (§5) — these are real target *companies*, not just a search filter.
- **San Diego angle:** start local (§5) — this is the one segment where a founder based in San Diego has a built-in, unfair advantage over any remote SDR (sales development rep).

Also add **Buying Intent** to every saved search where it's available — this is Apollo's signal (powered by a company called Bombora) that shows which companies are actively researching topics like "customer research," "voice of customer," or "call intelligence." You can add this on top of any segment above. ## 2. People Targeting

| Segment | Titles to put on the list | Seniority sweet spot | Who/what to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consultants / fractional operators | Founder, Principal, Partner, Owner, Fractional COO/CMO/CRO, Independent Consultant | They *are* the decision-maker — you only need to check that they run their own practice | Employees at a large consulting firm (they don't control what tools the firm uses) |
| Founders / product teams | Founder/CEO/Co-Founder (pre-seed to seed stage); Head of Product / VP Product / Director of Product (Series A and later); Head of User Research; Product Ops lead | Founder at companies under 30 people; Director/VP at companies of 30–200 people | A generic "Product Manager" at a company over 200 people — they usually don't control budget |
| Sales orgs | VP Sales, Head of Revenue / CRO, Director of Sales Enablement, Director/Head of RevOps | Director to VP level at companies of 50–500 people | C-suite executives at companies over 500 people — an executive assistant (EA) screens their inbox heavily; go one level down instead |
| Customer Success | VP Customer Success, Director of Customer Success, Director of CX, Head of Onboarding | Director to VP level at companies of 50–500 people | Same warning as Sales — avoid C-suite at companies over 500 people |
| Nonprofits | Executive Director/CEO (organizations under 20 staff); Director of Development, Director of Philanthropy, Chief Advancement Officer (larger organizations); Major Gifts Officer as a backup contact | Executive Director at small organizations is directly reachable; Development Director at mid-size or large organizations | Program-only staff with no budget authority; always search for several title variants at once — "development," "philanthropy," "advancement," and "fundraising" are used interchangeably across nonprofits |
| Event & community organizers | Event Director/Producer, Director of Community, Head of Programs, Sponsorship/Partnerships Director, Executive Director (membership organizations), Marketing Director (venues and DMCs — destination management companies), Chief Experience Officer (experience agencies) | Director level at agencies or venues with 2–100 people | A junior "Event Coordinator," unless you're also multi-threading (reaching out to several people at the company at once) under a Director |

**Avoiding gatekept titles — the general rule:** company size decides who you can actually reach — not the job title alone.
- **Under 50 employees:** a Director-level title usually *is* the real decision-maker, and reads their own inbox. Go straight to them.
- **50–500 employees:** target Director or VP, not C-suite. At this size, C-suite inboxes are the most heavily screened by an assistant. A Director or Head-of role has both real authority and an inbox you can actually reach.
- **Over 500 employees:** contact more than one person — one VP/Director, plus one manager-level "user" of the product. If you only have one contact, the deal dies the moment that person changes roles. ([Smarte — gatekeepers](https://www.smarte.pro/blog/get-past-the-gatekeeper), [Koncert — cold-calling C-suite](https://www.koncert.com/blog/cold-calling-c-suite-beat-gatekeepers-reach-executives))
- **Check the real scope of the role — don't trust the title alone.** Creative or inflated titles ("Chief Happiness Officer," "Growth Hacker") make Apollo's title search harder and can hide how much real authority someone has. Cross-check company size and a second signal (a recent hire, funding round, or job posting) before trusting the title alone.

**Which template to use (see `templates.md`):** consultants/fractional operators → *cold-consultant*. PMF founders reacting to a post or a new hire → *trigger-post*. Anyone found through a LinkedIn survey/poll (§4) → *survey-respondent* (our strongest template). Anyone reached through a referral (overlap with Rady, EvoNexus, or CONNECT, §5) → *referral-intro*.

## 3. Building the List in Apollo

**How to build the saved searches:**
1. Create one saved search per segment (using the filters from §1). Refresh it weekly. Apollo alerts you to new matches, so the list refills itself — you don't need to rebuild it from scratch.
2. Add **Buying Intent** and the right **job-change / job-posting** filter on top of every saved search. Firmographics alone give you a static list. Intent signals are what make the timing right.
3. Tag each export by segment, so you can trace replies back to the right segment and template in your weekly metrics review (§6).

**List cleanup — required before anything goes into a sequence:**
- **Only send to Apollo-verified email addresses.** Apollo labels contacts as Verified, Guessed, Unavailable, or Catch-all. Treat anything that isn't **Verified** as a separate, higher-risk group, and send to it more carefully.
- **Catch-all addresses** (a domain that accepts any email, so you can't tell if a specific address is real): about 40% of B2B contact records use a catch-all domain, and an *unverified* catch-all address bounces roughly 27 times more often than a verified one. Keep catch-all sends to just 2–5% of any batch, and only after checking by hand (LinkedIn, the company site); if catch-all bounces start rising, stop sending to that whole batch right away. ([Enrichley — catch-all](https://enrichley.com/blog/what-is-catch-all-email), [Findymail — catch-all](https://www.findymail.com/blog/what-are-catch-all-emails/))
- **Never send to role-based inboxes** (`info@`, `sales@`, `hello@`, `contact@`) — they almost never turn into replies, and they can hurt your spam score.
- **Remove duplicates across segments.** If a contact shows up in two saved searches (say, a consultant who also organizes events), put them in only one sequence — whichever fits them better.

**Realistic list size for 20 sends a day — the detail most people miss:** 20 emails a day is your **total daily budget — new first-touches plus scheduled follow-ups combined**. This matches the safe range that the deliverability (whether your email lands in the inbox vs. spam) research below agrees on: roughly 20–40 sends a day per warmed-up mailbox. It is **not** 20 brand-new contacts every single day forever:
- **Week 1:** your pipeline is empty, so most of the 20 daily sends can be new first-touches (about 100 new contacts a week).
- **Week 3 and after (steady state):** with a 4–6 touch sequence spread over 10–21 days, earlier weeks' follow-ups now compete for the same 20-send budget. In practice this settles to about **8–10 new first-touches a day** (40–50 a week), with the remaining 10–12 sends a day going to scheduled follow-ups.
- **Plan your list-building around this:** aim for about 200–250 net *sendable* contacts per month, per active segment (Verified, and not a role-based inbox) — not 400 or more. Since verification and cleanup (above) will drop some of any raw list, pull roughly **300–350 raw records** per segment per month to end up with that 200–250.
- Actively run only 2 segments at a time (for example, #1 and #2 from the priority table above), instead of spreading 20 sends a day across all six — thinner lists per segment just run out faster; it does not make your targeting better.

## 4. Hidden Best Practices That Actually Move Reply Rates

### Deliverability foundations (do this before sending anything)
- **Use a separate sending domain.** Never send cold email from your main business domain. Instead, buy a lookalike domain just for outbound email, and connect it in Apollo. This protects your main domain's reputation if something goes wrong.
- **Set up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC correctly, all three together** (SPF/DKIM/DMARC = email settings that prove you're a real sender). DMARC ties SPF and DKIM together, and tells the receiving mail server what to do if a check fails. Google and Yahoo's 2026 rules for bulk senders require this, plus a one-click unsubscribe link on anything that looks like marketing email. ([Clay — B2B deliverability](https://www.clay.com/blog/b2b-cold-email-deliverability), [LeadHaste — domain setup](https://leadhaste.com/blog/cold-email-domain-setup-guide-2026))
- **Warm up the mailbox for 3–4 weeks before sending real emails.** A brand-new domain or mailbox needs at least 14–21 days to build up a sending history. Skipping this step is the single most common reason a technically correct setup still lands in spam. ([Unify GTM — cold email 2026](https://www.unifygtm.com/explore/cold-email-2026-domain-setup-deliverability-sequences))
- **Keep volume at about 20–30 sends a day per mailbox.** Your target of 20 a day is already inside the safe range — don't push it higher just because Apollo allows more. At 20 a day, you probably don't need to rotate between mailboxes at all. One warmed-up mailbox per sender (founder, intern) is enough. Add a second mailbox only as a backup.
- **Make the first email look and feel like plain text — no links, images, or tracking pixels.** Studies show that HTML-formatted cold emails bounce far more often than plain text ones in aggregate. Also, tracking whether someone opened an email requires hiding a small image (a "tracking pixel," an HTML `<img>` tag) inside it. Turn off open and click tracking on first-touch cold sends — it hurts deliverability, and it's also a giveaway that the email is a mail-merge, not a real note from a person. ([Warmy — plain text vs HTML](https://www.warmy.io/blog/plain-text-or-html-in-cold-email/), [Kalungi — plain text deliverability](https://www.kalungi.com/blog/plain-text-emails-inbox-deliverability))

### Send timing & cadence
- **Tuesday through Thursday, mid-morning, in the recipient's own time zone** — this is the sweet spot that shows up again and again across studies.
- **4–6 touches over 10–21 days** is the standard range. Apollo's own data points to **6 touches over 14 days**, mixing 3–4 emails with 1–2 LinkedIn or call steps — cadences that mix channels get more replies than email alone. Space the earliest follow-ups 2–3 days apart, and later ones 7–14 days apart. ([Apollo — sequence components](https://www.apollo.io/insights/what-are-the-most-important-components-of-a-successful-outbound-sales-sequence), [ModernInbound — sequencing](https://moderninbound.com/blog/cold-email-sequencing-how-many-touches-is-too-many))
- **Every follow-up must add something new** — a new angle, a new piece of proof, or a new question — never a bare "just checking in" or "circling back." Both phrases test badly in every 2026 benchmark study we reviewed, and they make the email read like a template, not a real person. ([LeadHaste — follow-up writing](https://leadhaste.com/blog/how-to-write-a-cold-email-follow-up))
- **Never fake a `Re:` or `Fwd:`** in a follow-up subject line when there's no real earlier thread — it might raise your open rate briefly, but it gets flagged as both a trust problem and a deliverability problem.

### Subject-line craft — and a deliberate deviation from generic advice
- Most 2026 advice agrees: short, plain phrasing ("quick question," "intro re: [company]") beats clever or long subject lines, and personalizing the subject line raises open rates by roughly 50%. ([Mixmax — subject lines](https://www.mixmax.com/blog/cold-email-subject-lines-2026))
- **But this project's own drafting rules ban generic subject lines** like "Quick question" or "Following up" (set in `outbound_email.baml`). Instead, we require specific, human ones — and `templates.md`'s actual subject lines follow that rule ("your discovery calls → evidence you can cite," not "quick question"). **Follow this internal rule**: specific and concrete beats generic-but-proven, because a specific subject line *is* a personalization signal in itself, and once a prospect gets a second "quick question" email from the same sender, it starts to look like a template, not a person.

### The personalization bar
- **One real, specific, sourced hook — or don't send.** This is already the hard rule in `templates.md` and in the BAML gate (an internal AI safety check that flags `needs_more_research=true` instead of sending a padded, generic draft) — the data backs this up strongly: a generic name/company mail-merge gets the usual 1–5% reply rate, while campaigns where the prospect feels genuinely understood report **15–30%**, and the top 10% of highly personalized B2B campaigns are documented at **8–15%** reply. ([Sendr.ai — high reply rate data](https://www.sendr.ai/blog/high-reply-rate-cold-email-data-2026), [Hypergen — personalization tactics](https://www.hypergen.io/blog/cold-email-personalization-tactics))
- The hook has to connect to *their* specific role, a recent trigger (funding, a new hire, a post, an event), or a visible pain point — not their industry in general. "You're in SaaS" is not a hook; "you just hired a Product Researcher" is.

### LinkedIn pre-warming a list
- Research supports engaging with someone on LinkedIn before emailing them: one documented cadence — connect on day 1, email on day 3, LinkedIn message on day 5, email on day 8 — gets **3–4 times** the reply rate of using either channel alone. ([Overloop — LinkedIn vs. email](https://overloop.com/blog/linkedin-vs-email-which-performs-better-for-b2b-outreach))
- **UpSight already has a sharper version of this built into `templates.md`: the "survey-respondent" dogfood template** (a "dogfood" template is one that uses our own product to generate the outreach itself). Run a short, real, public LinkedIn poll/survey inside a target segment (e.g., "How do you currently turn customer interviews into decisions?") a week or two before a cold batch:
  - Anyone who **responds** becomes a warm lead for the *survey-respondent* template — internally flagged as "the strongest play we have," because the email demonstrates the product on data the prospect helped create, and Apollo/generic AI writers can't produce it.
  - Anyone who **votes or comments but doesn't fully respond** still gives you a free, real personalization hook for the surrounding cold batch ("saw the range of answers on my poll about X — you clearly deal with this too").
  - The poll's aggregate result becomes proof content you can reference in the hook itself.
  - *Caveat:* the LinkedIn-engagement-before-email lift above is independently benchmarked; the specific "run a poll" mechanic is this project's own tactic (already encoded in `templates.md`), not a separately sourced statistic — flagging that distinction rather than overclaiming external validation for it.

### Turning local into replies — without over-asking
- **On a cold first touch, local is a rapport hook — not a meeting request.** "Fellow San Diego founder" or a shared local reference lowers the barrier and signals a real person, not a mail-merge — but keep the actual ask low-friction (a reply, a quick look, a 2-min example), exactly as `templates.md` does. A stranger won't invest an hour in coffee before there's any relationship; leading a cold email with "grab coffee this week?" asks for more than a first touch has earned.
- **Coffee/in-person is a conversion step you *earn* — offer it after a positive reply, not before.** Once someone engages, *"want to grab coffee near [Gaslamp / Little Italy / UTC]? I'm local"* converts far better than a Calendly link — and it's a close a remote SDR structurally can't make. That's where the San Diego advantage actually cashes in: on the second move, not the first.
- **Events are how you skip cold entirely.** Attend one recurring local event a week (CONNECT/SDVG, EvoNexus office hours, an sdtechscene.org meetup, a Rady Founders' Society gathering). Meeting someone in person *first* turns the follow-up into a warm email ("we were both at [event] last week") — and an in-person coffee ask is completely normal once you've actually met.
- **Use San Diego Startup Week (Nov 2–6, 2026) as your annual anchor** — pre-event outreach to the speaker/exhibitor list to book on-site meetings, then post-event follow-up referencing the actual session or booth, never a generic "great meeting you."
- **For event & community organizers specifically:** become the case study — shoot receipts + a highlight clip for one real local event this year (an SD Brewers Guild festival, a smaller SD Startup Week satellite event), then use it as the cold hook for every other producer next year: *"we shot this for [local event] — want the same for yours?"*

### Local hooks that lift replies (rapport openers, not meeting asks)
- "fellow San Diego founder — quick one"
- "saw you're speaking at SD Startup Week"
- "EvoNexus alum? quick q" / "fellow Rady alum — quick q" (only if genuinely true)
- Mention the specific local event you were both at, by name — never "a recent event"
- **Save "coffee near [neighborhood]?" for *after* they reply** — or for someone you actually met at an event. It's a strong move for a second touch, but weak as a first one.

## 5b. The LA / Orange County Play

([AFP Orange County](https://afpglobal.org/chapters/afp-ca-orange-county-california-chapter))

### Turning LA/OC into replies — without over-asking

- **The San Diego "I'm local, coffee this week" cold ask does not transfer here — don't try it.** The founder doesn't live in LA/OC, so opening cold with anything implying local presence is a claim a prospect can and will check, and it breaks trust the moment it doesn't hold up. The honest version is **"I'm down in San Diego, up in LA/OC regularly"** — proximity and willingness to show up in person, without pretending to be a local.
- **Warm-from-event is the real lever, not cold-plus-local-flavor.** Only use an LA/OC "we were both there" hook *after* actually attending — LA Tech Week, OCIW, a Techstars/Grid110/TCA demo day. That makes the hook true instead of aspirational, and it's the same mechanic that makes San Diego events work, just requiring an actual trip instead of a walk down the street.
- **Referral paths do the same job cold email can't.** TCA/OCTANe/Techstars/Grid110 portfolio overlap is a legitimate warm-intro path, same category as the EvoNexus/CONNECT overlap in San Diego — weaker on raw density, but real.
- **Batch the trip, and say so.** Since it's a drive, not a stroll, don't burn a round trip on one meeting — stack 3–5 booked touches (meetings, a demo day, an event) around a single day up in LA/OC, and make that explicit in the ask: *"I'll be up for LA Tech Week the week of Oct 12 — happy to grab 20 minutes in person, or a call works too if that's easier."* The optionality (in-person or call) keeps the ask low-friction; the specificity signals it's a real trip, not a fishing line.
- **Coffee is still a second-touch move here, not a first-touch one — just phrased for distance.** After a reply: *"I'm up in LA that week anyway — want to grab coffee, or happy to jump on a call"* earns the same conversion lift San Diego's in-person ask gets, without overclaiming home-field status.

### Local hooks that lift replies (rapport openers, not meeting asks)

- "saw you're speaking/exhibiting at LA Tech Week" / "at OCIW"
- "congrats on the Techstars LA / Grid110 / TCA raise" (only once it's public)
- "USC / UCLA / UCI alum? quick q" (only if genuinely true)
- "I'm down in San Diego, up in LA/OC regularly — quick one" (the honest SoCal-adjacent framing — never "I'm local")
- Referencing the specific demo day, panel, or booth you were both at, by name — never "a recent LA event"
- **Save "coffee/coworking session next time I'm up" for *after* they reply, or for someone actually met at an event** — same rule as San Diego, just anchored to a trip instead of a neighborhood.

## 6. Metrics — Track Weekly

| Metric | Formula | Healthy benchmark (2026) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sends** | count | about 20/day (about 100/week) total, new + follow-up | This is your volume ceiling from §3/§4 — don't chase a higher number |
| **Bounce rate** | bounces ÷ sends | **under 3%** is healthy; **over 5%** means stop and fix the list | Domain reputation drops fast once you're above 5% |
| **Reply rate (all)** | replies ÷ sends | 3–6% broad B2B baseline · 6–8% well-targeted · 8%+ intent-driven | One-to-one, founder-written emails with real personalization should land you in the higher bands, not the floor |
| **Positive-reply rate** | positive replies ÷ sends | 1.5–3% typical · 4–5%+ top decile · hyper-personalized campaigns documented 8–15% | **This is the number that actually matters** — total reply rate includes "not interested," this doesn't |
| **Meetings booked rate** | meetings ÷ sends | 0.5–2.5%, ~2.3% reported average (Q1 2026) | Translate to a real weekly target below |
| **Meetings/week (absolute)** | — | Baseline ~2/week at 100 sends/week and a 2% rate; **target 3–5/week** given hyper-personalization + the San Diego warm-intro path | This is the number to report out, not a percentage |

**Weekly review ritual:**
1. Break every metric above down **by segment**, not just as one overall number — a strong segment hiding inside an average is exactly what you'd miss otherwise.
2. Retire or pause the worst-performing segment each week; give its share of the 20-a-day budget to whichever segment is outperforming.
3. Track reply rate **by template** too (`templates.md`'s five skeletons) — this is something you can test and change same-day, unlike list quality.
4. Bounce rate is a kill switch, not a KPI to average over time — act the week it crosses 5%, don't wait for the monthly rollup.
